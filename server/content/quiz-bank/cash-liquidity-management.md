# Quiz

1. {#q1} What is the core problem liquidity management addresses?
   - [x] Holding enough readily available cash to meet near-term obligations without a forced, poorly timed sale of other assets
   - Maximizing the total return of a portfolio regardless of cash needs
   - Eliminating all cash holdings from a portfolio
   - Liquidity management has no connection to meeting obligations
   > The core tension is having cash available exactly when it's needed, so obligations don't force an untimely sale of other assets.

2. {#q2} What does cash forecasting help a company determine?
   - [x] The minimum cash buffer actually needed, based on projected inflows and outflows over a coming horizon
   - The company's stock price over the next year
   - A fixed cash target that never needs to be updated
   - Cash forecasting is unrelated to liquidity management
   > Forecasting expected cash flows lets a company estimate a real, needs-based buffer instead of guessing at an arbitrary cash target.

3. {#q3} What does a sweep account do?
   - [x] Automatically moves cash above a threshold into an interest-bearing instrument overnight and back the next morning
   - Permanently locks cash away for five years
   - Converts all cash holdings into stock
   - Sweep accounts eliminate the need for any cash forecasting
   > A sweep account puts idle cash to work overnight without sacrificing next-day access, capturing yield that would otherwise be lost.

4. {#q4} What is the risk of holding too little liquidity?
   - [x] Being forced into a poorly timed asset sale, or an outright default, during a period of stress
   - Earning too much interest income
   - There is no risk to holding too little liquidity
   - Being unable to ever spend any cash at all
   > Insufficient liquidity is exactly what forces bad timing decisions — selling assets or missing payments — when obligations come due unexpectedly.

5. {#q5} What is effective liquidity management actually optimizing?
   - [x] The tradeoff between the cost of holding too much idle cash and the risk of holding too little
   - Eliminating cash holdings entirely to maximize returns
   - Holding the maximum possible amount of cash at all times
   - There is no tradeoff involved in liquidity management
   > Neither extreme is efficient — the goal is a right-sized cash buffer that covers real needs without unnecessarily sacrificing return.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A company holds $6 million of cash. Its 13-week forecast shows net outflows of $4 million, and it keeps a 10% buffer on top. How much can it invest in short-term instruments?
   - [x] $1.6 million
   - $2.0 million
   - $0.4 million
   - $2.4 million
   > Cash to keep liquid is $4M × 1.10 = $4.4M, so the amount available to invest is $6M − $4.4M = $1.6M.

7. {#calc2} [calc] A company's forecast shows net outflows of $0.6 million a week for 13 weeks, and it holds a 15% buffer. It has $10 million of cash. How much can it invest?
   - About $2.2 million
   - About $1.5 million
   - [x] About $1.03 million
   - About $8.97 million
   > The needed cash is 13 × $0.6M = $7.8M, and with the buffer $7.8M × 1.15 = $8.97M. The rest, $10M − $8.97M ≈ $1.03M, can be invested.

8. {#bk1} [calc] A firm has $2 million of idle cash that earns 4% (365-day basis) in a sweep account for 90 days. What interest does it earn?
   - $20,000
   - [x] $19,726
   - $80,000
   - $6,575
   > Interest = $2,000,000 × 4% × 90 / 365 = $19,726.

9. {#bk2} [calc] A company has $1.5 million of current assets and $1.0 million of current liabilities. What is its current ratio?
   - 0.67
   - 0.5
   - 2.5
   - [x] 1.5
   > Current ratio = current assets / current liabilities = $1.5 million / $1.0 million = 1.5.
