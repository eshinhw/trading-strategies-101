# Quiz

1. {#q1} What does the hedging pressure hypothesis claim about commodity producers?
   - Producers have no incentive to use futures markets at all
   - [x] Producers have a natural incentive to sell futures contracts to hedge their future production, creating chronic selling pressure
   - Producers always buy futures contracts rather than selling them
   - Producers are legally required to avoid futures markets
   > The theory holds that producers hedge future output by selling futures, and this systematic selling pressure is theorized to push futures prices below expected future spot prices.

2. {#q2} How does hedging pressure relate to the roll-yield/backwardation-contango framework?
   - The two concepts are completely unrelated
   - [x] Hedging pressure offers an economic explanation for why a given curve shape (like backwardation) exists in the first place, while roll yield describes the return that results from that shape
   - Hedging pressure and roll yield are exactly the same concept
   - Hedging pressure only applies to stock markets, not commodities
   > Roll yield is a mechanical description of the return from a curve shape, while hedging pressure is a proposed economic driver behind why that curve shape (often backwardation) tends to occur.

3. {#q3} In which commodities does the hedging pressure trading strategy look for the strongest opportunity?
   - Commodities where producers and speculators are perfectly balanced
   - [x] Commodities where a small number of producers dominate hedging activity and speculators are relatively scarce
   - Commodities with no futures market at all
   - Only commodities that have never experienced any price volatility
   > The strategy targets markets where producer-driven selling pressure is likely strongest relative to the speculative capital available to absorb it, theorizing a larger structural premium in those cases.

4. {#q4} What position does a hedging-pressure strategy typically take in the identified markets?
   - Short futures positions, betting prices will fall further
   - [x] Long futures positions, acting as the counterparty that absorbs producers' hedging demand
   - No position at all — the strategy only observes hedging activity
   - A position in the commodity's underlying equity, not its futures
   > The strategy goes long futures to be the counterparty for producers' hedging-driven selling, aiming to capture the theorized structural discount.

5. {#q5} What is the main risk to a hedging-pressure strategy?
   - There is no risk once the strategy is implemented
   - [x] The producer-driven imbalance can shift or reverse if consumers become the dominant hedgers instead, flipping the pressure in the opposite direction
   - Hedging pressure is fixed permanently and can never change for any commodity
   - The strategy requires taking on unlimited physical delivery risk
   > If the underlying market structure changes — for example, consumers rather than producers becoming the dominant hedgers — the theorized bias in futures pricing can reverse, working against a strategy built on the original assumption.

6. {#calc1} [calc] Producers' selling pushes corn futures to $6.25 while the expected future spot price is $6.40. What is the expected gain per 5,000-bushel long contract?
   - [x] $750
   - $150
   - $31,250
   - $32,000
   > The expected premium is $6.40 − $6.25 = $0.15 a bushel, and $0.15 × 5,000 = $750.

7. {#bk1} [calc] In a futures market, hedgers hold 120,000 short contracts and 80,000 long contracts, and open interest is 400,000. What is hedgers' net short position as a share of open interest?
   - 30%
   - 50%
   - [x] 10%
   - 40,000
   > Net short = 120,000 − 80,000 = 40,000. As a share of open interest: 40,000 / 400,000 = 10%.

8. {#bk2} [calc] Hedgers are net short 40,000 contracts. Speculators take the other side of the whole market. What is speculators' net position?
   - [x] Net long 40,000 contracts
   - Net short 40,000 contracts
   - Net long 80,000 contracts
   - Flat
   > For every futures contract, a short has a long. If hedgers are net short 40,000, speculators are net long 40,000.

9. {#bk3} [calc] A hedging-pressure strategy buys futures when hedgers are net short more than 5% of open interest. Hedgers were net short 4% last month and 7% this month. What does the rule do this month?
   - Sells, because 7% is above 5%
   - Does nothing, because the change is only 3%
   - [x] Buys, because 7% is above 5%
   - Buys, because the change is above 5%
   > The rule tests the level against 5%. At 7% it is above the trigger, so the rule buys.
