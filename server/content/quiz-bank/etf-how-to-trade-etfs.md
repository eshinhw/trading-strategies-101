# Quiz

1. {#q1} What order types can be used to trade an ETF?
   - [x] The same order types used for individual stocks — market orders, limit orders, and so on
   - Only market orders are ever allowed
   - ETFs require a completely different, specialized order system
   - ETFs cannot be bought or sold using orders at all
   > Since ETF shares trade like stocks on an exchange, the same familiar order types apply directly.

2. {#q2} What influences an ETF's bid-ask spread, beyond how heavily the ETF itself trades?
   - [x] How liquid the ETF's underlying holdings are
   - The ETF's ticker symbol length
   - Only the time of day, with no other factors
   - The color scheme of the ETF issuer's website
   > Market makers can source or offload the underlying basket via creation/redemption, so the liquidity of those underlying holdings feeds directly into how tight the ETF's own spread can be.

3. {#q3} What does it mean if an ETF is trading at a "premium" to its NAV?
   - [x] Its market price is trading above the per-share value of its actual underlying holdings
   - Its market price is trading below its underlying value
   - The ETF has stopped trading entirely
   - The ETF has no underlying holdings at all
   > A premium means the market price sits above NAV — the arbitrage mechanism from creation/redemption usually keeps this gap small and temporary.

4. {#q4} What is an ETF's expense ratio?
   - [x] An annual fee, as a percentage of assets, deducted directly from the fund's returns
   - A one-time fee paid only when first buying shares
   - A fee charged exclusively to authorized participants
   - A tax paid to the government on every trade
   > The expense ratio is an ongoing annual cost taken out of the fund's returns, making it a useful way to compare the cost of similar ETFs.

5. {#q5} Why does comparing expense ratios matter when choosing between similar ETFs?
   - [x] It's one of the simplest ways to compare the cost of ETFs tracking the same index
   - Expense ratios are identical across every ETF, so comparison is pointless
   - Expense ratios only apply to actively managed mutual funds, never ETFs
   - Lower expense ratios always guarantee higher returns
   > Since expense ratios directly reduce returns over time, comparing them across similar index-tracking ETFs is a straightforward way to judge relative cost.

6. {#calc1} [calc] An ETF quotes $25.00 bid and $25.04 ask. How much does it cost to buy 4,000 shares and sell them straight back?
   - [x] $160
   - $40
   - $100,160
   - $0.04
   > Buying at the ask and selling at the bid loses the spread: $0.04 × 4,000 shares = $160.

7. {#calc2} [calc] An ETF trades at $100.40 and its NAV is $100.00. What is the premium?
   - 0.04%
   - 4%
   - [x] 0.4%
   - $0.40
   > The premium is ($100.40 − $100.00) / $100.00 = 0.4%.

8. {#bk1} [calc] An ETF trades at a $0.04 bid-ask spread and a $80 share price. What is the spread as a percentage of price?
   - [x] 0.05%
   - 0.5%
   - 0.04%
   - 5%
   > $0.04 / $80 = 0.0005, or 0.05% (5 basis points).

9. {#bk2} [calc] An investor places a market order for 500 ETF shares quoted $30.00 bid / $30.06 ask. How much do they pay at the quoted ask?
   - $15,000
   - $15,015
   - [x] $15,030
   - $14,970
   > A market buy fills at the ask: 500 × $30.06 = $15,030.

10. {#bk3} [calc] An ETF quotes $99.98 bid and $100.02 ask. A trader buys 1,000 shares at the ask. How much is paid above the midpoint?
   - [x] $20
   - $40
   - $2
   - $200
   > The midpoint is $100.00. $0.02 × 1,000 = $20.

11. {#bk4} [calc] An ETF closes at $97.40 and its NAV is $98.00. What is the discount to NAV?
   - 0.6 cents
   - 6.1%
   - 0.06%
   - [x] 0.61%
   > ($98.00 − $97.40) / $98.00 = 0.61%.

12. {#bk5} [calc] An investor wants to invest $30,000 in an ETF with an ask of $50.00. How many whole shares can be bought?
   - 60
   - [x] 600
   - 6,000
   - 30
   > $30,000 / $50 = 600 shares.

13. {#bk6} [calc] A trader buys 500 ETF shares at the ask and sells at the bid, with a $0.06 spread. What does the round trip cost?
   - $3
   - $300
   - $0.06
   - [x] $30
   > $0.06 × 500 = $30.

14. {#bk7} [calc] An investor places a buy limit order at $100.00 when the ask is $100.02. Will it fill immediately?
   - Yes, at $100.02
   - [x] No, it fills only if the ask falls to $100.00 or lower
   - Yes, at $100.00
   - No, limit orders cannot be placed on ETFs
   > A buy limit order fills at the limit price or better. The ask is above it, so the order waits.

15. {#bk8} [calc] A 10 basis point round-trip spread cost is paid on an ETF held for 2 years. What is that cost per year?
   - [x] 0.05%
   - 0.10%
   - 0.2%
   - 0.50%
   > 0.10% / 2 = 0.05% a year.

16. {#bk9} What do ETFs use to place trades?
   - Special order types available only to institutions
   - Only market orders
   - [x] The same order types as stocks, such as market and limit orders
   - Only orders placed at the close
   > Because ETF shares trade on an exchange, the same order types and considerations apply.

17. {#bk10} What influences an ETF's bid-ask spread besides its own trading volume?
   - The color of the fund company's logo
   - [x] How liquid its underlying holdings are, since market makers can hedge using them
   - The investor's account size
   - The day of the week only
   > Market makers can lean on the underlying securities' liquidity, so ETFs of liquid holdings often trade with narrow spreads.

18. {#bk11} When can an ETF's market price differ most from its NAV?
   - Never, because the two are identical by law
   - Only at 4pm each day
   - Only when the ETF pays a dividend
   - [x] With less liquid ETFs or during volatile markets, although creation and redemption normally keep the gap small
   > A premium or discount can open briefly when trading conditions are stressed.
