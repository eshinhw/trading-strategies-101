# Quiz

1. {#bk1} [calc] An investor owns 500 shares of a company with 20 million shares outstanding. What percentage of the company does the investor own?
   - 0.025%
   - 0.25%
   - [x] 0.0025%
   - 2.5%
   > 500 / 20,000,000 = 0.000025, or 0.0025%.

2. {#bk2} [calc] A company earns $40 million and an investor owns 5% of its shares. What is the investor's proportional claim on those profits?
   - [x] $2 million
   - $200,000
   - $20 million
   - $8 million
   > 5% × $40 million = $2 million. The profit is the company's, but each share carries a proportional claim on it.

3. {#bk3} [calc] An investor buys 200 shares at $40 and the company fails, leaving the shares worthless. Because of limited liability, what is the most the investor can lose?
   - $40
   - $80,000
   - [x] $8,000
   - More than $8,000, because creditors can pursue the investor
   > The most a shareholder can lose is what was paid: 200 × $40 = $8,000.

4. {#bk4} [calc] A company has 10 million shares, each with one vote. A shareholder owns 50,000 shares. What share of the vote does the shareholder hold?
   - 5%
   - [x] 0.5%
   - 0.05%
   - 50%
   > 50,000 / 10,000,000 = 0.5%.

5. {#bk5} How does owning a share differ from holding a company's bond?
   - A share is a loan with a fixed repayment, while a bond is an ownership stake
   - Both are fixed repayments, but a share pays more
   - A share has no claim on profits, while a bond does
   - [x] A share is a fractional ownership stake with no fixed repayment, while a bond is a loan owed a fixed repayment
   > Shareholders own a proportional slice of what the company owns and earns, with no promised payment. Bondholders are creditors owed a fixed sum.

6. {#bk6} What is a stock's price at any moment?
   - The value set each day by the company's board
   - [x] Whatever the market is currently willing to pay for one share, reset by real buy and sell orders
   - The company's book value divided by shares outstanding
   - The average price of the last year
   > The price reflects the market's collective, ever-changing view of the company's future prospects.
