# Quiz

1. {#q1} What does a target company's stock typically do immediately after a merger is announced?
   - It falls to zero immediately
   - [x] It jumps toward the announced deal price, but usually still trades at a discount to it
   - It becomes completely untradeable
   - It always trades above the announced deal price
   > The stock rises toward the deal price on the announcement but typically trades below it, reflecting the market's assessment of the risk the deal might not close.

2. {#q2} What does merger arbitrage bet on?
   - That the acquiring company's stock will double in price
   - [x] That the announced deal will close, allowing the target's stock to converge to the full deal price
   - That the merger will definitely be rejected by regulators
   - That interest rates will fall
   > Merger arbitrage buys the target at its discount to the deal price, betting the deal successfully closes and the discount closes with it.

3. {#q3} In a stock-for-stock deal, what is the standard merger-arbitrage position structure?
   - Long the target's stock only, with no position in the acquirer
   - [x] Long the target's stock and short the acquirer's stock in the ratio specified by the deal terms
   - Short both the target's and the acquirer's stock
   - Long the acquirer's stock only
   > Shorting the acquirer in the deal's specified ratio hedges out the acquirer's own stock-price moves, isolating the bet purely on whether the deal itself closes.

4. {#q4} What is the primary risk in merger arbitrage?
   - The risk that the stock market closes permanently
   - [x] Deal-completion risk — the merger falling apart due to antitrust challenges, financing issues, or a failed shareholder vote
   - The risk that dividends are paid too frequently
   - The risk that the target company's name changes
   > If the deal collapses for any of several possible reasons, the target's stock typically falls sharply back toward its pre-announcement level, which is the strategy's main risk.

5. {#q5} Why is merger arbitrage's return profile often compared to selling insurance?
   - Because it involves literally selling insurance policies
   - [x] Because it collects a steady stream of small, positive spreads across many deals, punctuated by occasional sharp losses when a deal breaks
   - Because it guarantees a fixed return with no risk at all
   - Because it can only be used by insurance companies
   > Like selling insurance, the strategy earns small, consistent premiums (the deal spread) most of the time, but faces occasional large losses when a "claim" (a broken deal) occurs — which is why diversifying across many deals matters.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A merger target trades at $47.50. The deal pays $50 with a 90% chance, and the shares would fall to $38 if it fails. What is the expected profit per share?
   - $2.50
   - [x] $1.30
   - $10.00
   - $0.00
   > The expected value is 0.90 × $50 + 0.10 × $38 = $48.80, so the expected profit is $48.80 − $47.50 = $1.30 per share. A small expected gain carries a large downside if the deal breaks.

7. {#bk1} [calc] A stock is offered at $50 per share in cash and trades at $48. What is the gross spread as a percentage of the current price?
   - [x] 4.17%
   - 4.0%
   - 2.0%
   - 8.33%
   > The spread is $50 − $48 = $2. As a percentage of $48 it is 4.17%.

8. {#bk2} [calc] A merger-arbitrage position earns a 4.17% spread and the deal is expected to close in 6 months. What is the simple annualized return?
   - 4.17%
   - 2.08%
   - [x] 8.33%
   - 16.7%
   > 4.17% × (12 / 6) = 8.33% on a simple basis.

9. {#bk3} [calc] A target trades at $48 against a $50 offer. If the deal breaks the stock falls to $38. What deal-completion probability is implied by the price?
   - [x] 83.3%
   - 96%
   - 50%
   - 76%
   > $48 = p × $50 + (1 − p) × $38 gives 48 − 38 = p × 12, so p = 10 / 12 = 83.3%.

10. {#bk4} [calc] A target's shareholders receive 0.8 shares of the acquirer for each target share. The acquirer trades at $50 and the target at $38. What is the gross spread per target share?
   - $12
   - $0.80
   - $40
   - [x] $2
   > The offer is worth 0.8 × $50 = $40. The spread is $40 − $38 = $2.

11. {#bk5} [calc] In a stock-for-stock deal each target share converts into 0.8 acquirer shares. A trader buys 10,000 target shares. How many acquirer shares should be sold short to hedge?
   - 10,000
   - [x] 8,000
   - 12,500
   - 800
   > Hedge ratio = 0.8 per target share, so 10,000 × 0.8 = 8,000 acquirer shares.

12. {#bk6} [calc] A merger-arbitrage trader earns $2 on a $38 target if the deal closes, and loses $9 if it collapses. What probability of completion is needed to break even?
   - 50%
   - 18.2%
   - 95%
   - [x] 81.8%
   > Break even: p × $2 = (1 − p) × $9, so p = 9 / 11 = 81.8%.

13. {#bk7} Why do merger-arbitrage returns resemble selling insurance?
   - Gains are large and uncapped when a deal closes
   - [x] Gains are small and capped when a deal closes, while a collapsed deal causes a comparatively large, sudden loss
   - The strategy has no downside
   - Returns do not depend on the deal completing
   > The target can only converge up to the deal price, while a failed deal can drop it a long way.
