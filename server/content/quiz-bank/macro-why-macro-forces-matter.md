# Quiz

1. {#q1} Why do macro forces matter across many asset classes at once?
   - Each asset class is completely independent
   - [x] Interest rates, inflation, and growth affect stocks, bonds, currencies, and commodities at the same time
   - Governments set every price
   - Only bonds are affected by the economy
   > Macroeconomic forces cut across markets, which is why they explain much of what happens in prices.

2. {#q2} What does a macro investor do first?
   - Pick an individual stock
   - [x] Form a view on the economy, then decide which market best expresses it
   - Ignore economic data
   - Wait until all markets agree
   > The approach is top-down: view first, instrument second.

3. {#q3} Why are the markets macro investors use practical for large positions?
   - They are very small
   - [x] Government bonds, major currencies, and stock indexes are very large and liquid
   - They never change in price
   - They are closed to institutions
   > Deep, liquid markets make it possible to trade large positions.

4. {#q4} Where do macro investors look for opportunities related to policy and imbalances?
   - Where markets have priced everything perfectly
   - [x] In the gap between what is priced in and what actually happens
   - In companies' marketing
   - Only in commodities
   > Mispricing arises when markets don't correctly anticipate policy changes or economic adjustments.

5. {#q5} Why can a correct macro view still lose money?
   - A correct view always makes money
   - Macro views cannot be correct
   - [x] The view can be early, and markets can move against a position for a long time before the economy catches up
   - Markets always follow the economy at once
   > Timing risk and leverage mean even a right view can produce losses along the way.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A view that rates rise is expressed by shorting $10 million of bonds (duration 8). Yields rise 0.5 points, but 0.4 points were already priced in earlier. How much is gained from the remaining move?
   - $400,000
   - $320,000
   - [x] $80,000
   - $40,000
   > Only 0.5 − 0.4 = 0.1 percentage point is left, and 8 × 0.1% × $10M = $80,000.

7. {#calc2} [calc] A bond yields 5% when inflation is 3%. What is the approximate real yield?
   - [x] About 2%
   - About 8%
   - About 15%
   - About 1.7%
   > The real yield is roughly the nominal yield minus inflation: 5% − 3% = 2%.

8. {#bk1} [calc] An asset returns 6% in a year when inflation is 4%. What is its approximate real return?
   - 10%
   - [x] 2%
   - 6%
   - 24%
   > Real return ≈ nominal return − inflation = 6% − 4% = 2%.
