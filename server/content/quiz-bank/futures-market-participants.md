# Quiz

1. {#q1} What distinguishes a hedger from a speculator?
   - [x] A hedger already has (or will have) real exposure to an asset and trades futures to reduce that risk; a speculator has no such exposure and trades purely for profit
   - A hedger always loses money, while a speculator always profits
   - Only speculators are allowed to trade on an exchange
   - There is no meaningful difference between the two
   > The defining difference is motive and underlying exposure: a hedger is offsetting a real risk it already has, while a speculator is taking on new risk purely for potential profit.

2. {#q2} How does a market maker typically profit?
   - From taking a strong directional bet on price
   - [x] From the small spread between its continuously quoted bid and ask prices, not from predicting price direction
   - By charging hedgers a fixed annual fee
   - Market makers do not profit — they trade only as a public service
   > A market maker earns the bid-ask spread by standing ready to trade both sides constantly, profiting from that spread rather than from being right about where price is headed.

3. {#q3} What role do arbitrageurs play in a futures market?
   - [x] They exploit price discrepancies between related markets, and in doing so push prices back toward their proper economic relationship
   - They set the exchange's official settlement price by decree
   - They only trade options, never futures
   - They exist purely to provide news commentary to other traders
   > By trading toward mispricings between a future and its underlying (or between related contracts), arbitrageurs are the mechanism that keeps cost-of-carry pricing and spread relationships anchored to reality.

4. {#q4} Why does a futures market need all four participant types to function well?
   - It doesn't — a market could function with only hedgers present
   - [x] Each role supplies something the others depend on: risk-transfer demand, risk-absorbing capital, continuous liquidity, and pricing discipline
   - Only market makers are actually necessary
   - Arbitrageurs and speculators are redundant with each other
   > Removing any one role weakens the market — without speculators hedgers have no counterparty, without market makers trades aren't instant, and without arbitrageurs prices can drift from fair value.

5. {#q5} In the soybean processor example, what role does the trend-following fund play by selling the same contract the processor is buying?
   - Hedger
   - [x] Speculator, absorbing the price risk the processor wants to shed
   - Arbitrageur
   - Regulator
   > The fund has no soybean exposure to hedge — it's trading a directional view, making it the speculator supplying the other side of the processor's hedge.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A speculator sells 10 soybean contracts of 5,000 bushels, and the price rises $0.80. What does the speculator lose?
   - [x] $40,000
   - $4,000
   - $400,000
   - $8,000
   > 10 × 5,000 bushels × $0.80 = $40,000.

7. {#bk1} [calc] Crude oil is $80 spot and carrying costs to delivery total $2.50, so the fair future is $82.50. The future trades at $84.00. An arbitrageur sells the future and buys spot. What is the profit per contract (1,000 barrels)?
   - [x] $1,500
   - $2,500
   - $4,000
   - $150
   > The future is $1.50 above fair value. $1.50 × 1,000 = $1,500 per contract.

8. {#bk2} [calc] A market maker buys at 99.95 and sells at 100.05 on 200 round trips a day, each of 10 contracts worth $100 per point. What is the gross spread income for the day?
   - $2,000
   - $200,000
   - [x] $20,000
   - $100
   > Each round trip earns 0.10 points × $100 × 10 contracts = $100. 200 round trips earn $20,000.

9. {#bk3} [calc] A farmer sells 50,000 bushels of corn futures at $6.20. At harvest the farmer sells the crop in the cash market at $5.70 and buys the futures back at $5.75. What total revenue does the farmer end up with?
   - [x] $307,500
   - $285,000
   - $310,000
   - $22,500
   > The cash sale brings 50,000 × $5.70 = $285,000. The futures gain is ($6.20 − $5.75) × 50,000 = $22,500. Total = $307,500, an effective $6.15 per bushel.

10. {#bk4} [calc] A speculator buys 5 corn contracts (5,000 bushels each) at $6.10 and sells at $6.34. What is the profit?
   - $1,200
   - $30,000
   - $120,000
   - [x] $6,000
   > The gain is $0.24 × 5,000 × 5 = $6,000.

11. {#bk5} How does a market maker make money?
   - From predicting whether prices will rise over the next year
   - [x] From the small spread between its bid and ask prices, not from a view on direction
   - From taking physical delivery of the underlying commodity
   - From collecting fees on every trade the exchange matches
   > A market maker quotes both sides continuously and earns the spread, which also provides the liquidity others rely on.

12. {#bk6} What do arbitrageurs do for a futures market?
   - They provide the physical storage for delivered commodities
   - They set daily price limits for the exchange
   - They take the opposite side of every hedger's trade at a fixed price
   - [x] They trade price gaps back toward fair value, keeping futures consistent with the spot price and cost of carry
   > By selling a rich future or buying a cheap one against the underlying, arbitrageurs push prices toward where the underlying economics say they should be.

13. {#bk7} What is a speculator in a futures market?
   - A trader who must take delivery of the underlying asset
   - [x] A trader with no underlying exposure who takes price risk for profit, giving hedgers someone to trade with
   - A trader who uses futures only to reduce an existing exposure
   - An exchange official who monitors trading
   > Speculators accept the risk hedgers want to shed, and their capital and activity make the market work.

14. {#bk8} What happens to a futures market if its market makers disappear?
   - [x] Immediacy suffers: spreads widen and it becomes harder to trade quickly at a fair price
   - Nothing changes, since hedgers can always find each other instantly
   - Prices stop changing altogether
   - The clearinghouse takes over quoting prices
   > Market makers supply the continuous two-sided quotes that let both hedgers and speculators trade at any moment. Without them the market is thinner and costlier to use.
