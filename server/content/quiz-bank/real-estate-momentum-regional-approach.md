# Quiz

1. {#q1} What does a regional real estate momentum strategy do?
   - Ranks individual tenants by creditworthiness
   - [x] Ranks metro areas or regions by trailing price appreciation and tilts capital toward the strongest recent performers
   - Only ever invests in the region with the worst recent performance
   - Ignores price history entirely
   > Regional momentum applies the same logic as single-stock price momentum, but at the level of a regional market: rank by trailing return, tilt toward the top performers.

2. {#q2} Why might momentum effects persist longer in regional real estate than in public stock markets?
   - Because real estate markets are more liquid and efficient than stock markets
   - [x] Because information diffuses more slowly and capital takes longer to flow in and reprice properties, given how infrequently real estate transacts
   - Because real estate prices update continuously throughout the day
   - Because real estate has no relationship to local economic conditions
   > Real estate's slower information diffusion and infrequent transactions, compared to a continuously repricing stock market, is part of why a regional price trend can persist for longer before fully correcting.

3. {#q3} What lookback period is typically used for a regional real estate momentum strategy, and why?
   - A few minutes, since real estate prices change that quickly
   - [x] A year or more, reflecting how slowly real estate prices actually move compared to public markets
   - Exactly one day, matched to stock market conventions
   - No lookback period is ever used
   > Because real estate prices move much more slowly than stock prices, a meaningful trailing-return signal typically requires a longer lookback window, often a year or more.

4. {#q4} What is the central risk of a regional real estate momentum strategy?
   - [x] Regions that have appreciated quickly can become overextended relative to fundamentals and reverse sharply, meaning the strategy can buy in right before the run ends
   - Regional real estate momentum has no risk at all
   - Real estate prices can never reverse once they start rising
   - The strategy only invests in regions that never change
   > A region that led the upswing due to fast appreciation can just as easily lead the downswing once demand cools or financing tightens — the same trend-following logic that drives gains can drive losses.

5. {#q5} How is the logic of regional real estate momentum related to single-stock price momentum?
   - They are unrelated concepts
   - [x] It's the same cross-sectional, rank-by-trailing-return logic, just applied at the level of a regional market instead of an individual stock
   - Regional momentum only works in the opposite direction of stock momentum
   - Stock momentum requires ranking regions, not stocks
   > Both strategies rank a set of assets — stocks in one case, regions in the other — by trailing performance and tilt toward the top performers; the underlying ranking logic carries over directly.

6. {#calc1} [calc] Region returns over the past year are A +9%, B +4% and C −2%. A momentum investor puts $3 million in the top region, which returns +6% next year. What is the gain?
   - $270,000
   - $120,000
   - $60,000
   - [x] $180,000
   > The top region is A, and 6% × $3,000,000 = $180,000.

7. {#bk1} [calc] A regional price index rises from 110 to 118 over 12 months. What is its 12-month momentum?
   - 8%
   - 6.78%
   - 0.73%
   - [x] 7.27%
   > (118 − 110) / 110 = 7.27%.

8. {#bk2} [calc] A regional momentum strategy holds the 2 best of 4 regions equally weighted. The 12-month price growth rates are North +7%, South +4%, East +2% and West −1%. Next year North earns +5% and South earns +3%. What is the portfolio return?
   - 3.5%
   - [x] 4%
   - 5%
   - 2.5%
   > The best two are North and South. Equal weights give (5% + 3%) / 2 = 4%.

9. {#bk3} [calc] Regional A rose 9% last year and regional B rose 3%. A strategy overweights the momentum winner: 70% in A and 30% in B. If both repeat last year's growth, what is the portfolio growth?
   - 6%
   - 9%
   - 5.4%
   - [x] 7.2%
   > 0.70 × 9% + 0.30 × 3% = 6.3% + 0.9% = 7.2%.
