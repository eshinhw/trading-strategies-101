# Quiz

1. {#q1} What does multi-asset trend following apply a single trend-following rule to?
   - Only a single stock
   - [x] A broad basket of ETFs spanning many different, largely uncorrelated asset classes, such as equities, bonds, commodities, and currencies
   - Only cryptocurrency ETFs
   - Only ETFs within a single sector
   > Rather than trading one market, multi-asset trend following applies the same trend rule identically across many different asset classes represented by liquid ETF proxies.

2. {#q2} What is the main rationale for spreading one trading rule across so many different asset classes?
   - To guarantee every asset class is always in a strong trend simultaneously
   - [x] Diversification of trend opportunity — at any given time, some asset classes in a broad basket are likely to be in a genuine, tradeable trend even if others aren't
   - To reduce the total number of positions held to just one
   - Asset classes never actually behave differently from each other
   > Since no single market trends all the time, spreading the same rule across many uncorrelated asset classes increases the chance that the strategy captures a trend wherever one happens to be occurring.

3. {#q3} Why does a diversified multi-asset trend portfolio tend to have a smoother overall return profile than applying the same rule to just one or two closely related markets?
   - Smoother returns are unrelated to the correlation between asset classes
   - [x] The underlying asset classes are relatively uncorrelated, so a losing position in one (like equities) doesn't necessarily coincide with a losing position in another (like bonds or commodities)
   - All asset classes are perfectly correlated by definition
   - The strategy always holds exactly one position at a time
   > Because different asset classes often respond to different underlying drivers, their trend-following returns tend not to move in lockstep, smoothing the overall portfolio's return relative to trading a single market.

4. {#q4} Why can multi-asset trend following sometimes perform well during equity market crises?
   - It is legally prohibited from trading during crises
   - [x] A sustained equity downtrend is still a trend the strategy can trade (e.g., going short or to cash), while it may simultaneously catch rising trends in other asset classes like bonds or gold
   - Trend-following strategies automatically shut down during any market stress
   - Equity crises always cause every asset class to fall together with no exceptions
   > Since trends can form in either direction and across many markets, the strategy isn't dependent on equities specifically rising — it can capture downtrends in equities and uptrends elsewhere during the same crisis period.

5. {#q5} Why is multi-asset trend following often framed as a portfolio diversifier rather than purely a standalone return source?
   - Because it has historically performed identically to a simple buy-and-hold equity portfolio at all times
   - [x] Because it has sometimes performed well precisely during periods when equity buy-and-hold performs poorly, due to its ability to trade trends across many uncorrelated markets and directions
   - Because it can only be used by institutional investors
   - Because it requires no capital to implement
   > Its ability to find and trade trends outside of equities, including during equity selloffs, gives it a return pattern that can complement rather than mirror a traditional stock-heavy portfolio, which is why it's often used as a diversifying allocation.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A trend rule puts $10,000 in each of six ETFs and is long four of them, which return +3%, +1%, −2% and +4% for the month (the other two are in cash). What is the monthly profit?
   - $300
   - [x] $600
   - $2,600
   - $60
   > The long returns add to 3 + 1 − 2 + 4 = 6%, and 6% × $10,000 = $600.

7. {#bk1} [calc] A trend-following rule is applied to 6 ETFs from different asset classes. 4 are in uptrends and the rule holds each uptrend at an equal 1/6 weight, with the rest in cash. How much of the portfolio is invested?
   - 100%
   - 33.3%
   - [x] 66.7%
   - 16.7%
   > 4 positions × 1/6 = 66.7% invested, with 33.3% in cash.

8. {#bk2} [calc] Four uncorrelated ETF trend positions each have a volatility of 12% and are equally weighted. What is the volatility of the portfolio?
   - [x] 6%
   - 12%
   - 3%
   - 48%
   > With uncorrelated equal weights, volatility falls with the square root of the number of positions: 12% / √4 = 6%.

9. {#bk3} [calc] A multi-asset trend rule sizes each ETF by target volatility divided by its own volatility. The target is 10% and an ETF has a volatility of 20%. What weight does it get?
   - 200%
   - 10%
   - [x] 50%
   - 20%
   > Weight = 10% / 20% = 50%.

10. {#bk4} [calc] A trend rule is applied to 8 ETFs with equal weights of 1/8 each. Five are in uptrends and are held long, and the other three are in cash. How much of the portfolio is invested?
   - 100%
   - [x] 62.5%
   - 37.5%
   - 5%
   > 5 × 1/8 = 62.5% is invested and 37.5% is in cash.

11. {#bk5} [calc] Three ETFs have volatilities of 5%, 10% and 20%. A portfolio weights them in proportion to the inverse of volatility. What weight goes to the 5% volatility ETF?
   - 33.3%
   - 71.4%
   - 14.3%
   - [x] 57.1%
   > Inverse volatilities are 20, 10 and 5, summing to 35. 20 / 35 = 57.1%.

12. {#bk6} [calc] A trend strategy targets 10% volatility per position. An ETF has a volatility of 25%. What weight does it get?
   - 250%

   > Weight = 10% / 25% = 40%.

13. {#bk7} [calc] A trend portfolio holds four ETFs with equal 25% weights. They return +6%, +2%, −3% and 0%. What is the portfolio return?
   - 5%

   - 2%
   - [x] 1.25%
   > 0.25 × (6 + 2 − 3 + 0) = 0.25 × 5 = 1.25%.
