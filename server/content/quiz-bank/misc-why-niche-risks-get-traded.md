# Quiz

1. {#q1} Why do some businesses need specialized instruments to hedge?
   - They want to speculate on stock prices
   - [x] They face risks, such as weather or the gap between fuel and power prices, that ordinary securities don't cover
   - They have no risks
   - Governments forbid ordinary hedging
   > Exposures like weather or input-output margins have no standard security whose price tracks them.

2. {#q2} What does a hedger do in a risk transfer?
   - Keeps all the risk
   - [x] Pays, through a premium or fixed payments, to pass part of the risk to a counterparty
   - Receives only profits
   - Sets the market price
   > The hedger gives up some return to move the risk to someone willing to bear it.

3. {#q3} Why might an investor want to take on a weather or similar risk?
   - Because it always profits
   - [x] The risk may be unrelated to stocks and bonds, which can diversify a portfolio, and it pays a premium
   - Because it is required by law
   - Because it has no risk
   > Risks that move independently of markets can improve diversification.

4. {#q4} Why are these markets relatively small and illiquid?
   - They are banned
   - [x] Each exposure is specific, contracts are tailored, and few participants know how to price them
   - Everyone trades them
   - Stocks are forbidden
   > Tailored contracts and a small set of participants limit trading volume.

5. {#q5} What is basis risk in this context?
   - The risk that markets are closed on weekends
   - [x] The risk that the hedge instrument's payout doesn't match the actual loss, such as an index not matching local weather
   - The risk of a bank failing
   - The risk of printing money
   > Basis risk is the gap between the hedge's payout and the exposure being hedged.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A ski resort loses $80,000 for each warm day above 18, and a derivative pays $80,000 per warm day above 18. There are 30 warm days and the premium was $90,000. What is the resort's net result from the lost revenue, payout and premium?
   - $0
   - −$960,000
   - +$870,000
   - [x] −$90,000
   > The lost revenue is 12 × $80,000 = $960,000 and the payout is also $960,000, so only the $90,000 premium is left as a cost.

7. {#calc2} [calc] A weather seller collects a $120,000 premium on a contract with a $2 million cap. There is a 5% chance of a full payout and no payout otherwise. What is the expected profit?
   - $120,000
   - [x] $20,000
   - $100,000
   - $1,880,000
   > The expected payout is 5% × $2,000,000 = $100,000, so the expected profit is $120,000 − $100,000 = $20,000.

8. {#bk1} [calc] A catastrophe bond pays a 7% coupon on $1,000 and loses all principal with a 2% annual probability of a qualifying event. What is the expected annual return (ignoring recovery)?
   - 7%
   - [x] 4.9%
   - −2%
   - 9%
   > Expected return = 98% × 7% + 2% × (−100%) = 6.86% − 2% = 4.86%, about 4.9%.
