# Quiz

1. {#q1} What role does a futures exchange play as a self-regulatory organization?
   - [x] It writes and enforces its own rulebook — contract specs, position limits, disciplinary action, and price limits — alongside government regulation
   - It has no rule-making authority of its own
   - It replaces the need for any government regulator
   - It only regulates clearinghouses, not its own members
   > Exchanges maintain front-line, market-specific rules and enforcement that work alongside, not instead of, broader government oversight.

2. {#q2} What does a broker's suitability obligation typically involve?
   - [x] Considering whether futures trading fits a given customer's financial situation and experience before opening an account
   - Guaranteeing the customer will make money
   - Refusing to disclose any risks to avoid alarming customers
   - Requiring every customer to trade the same contracts
   > Suitability checks are meant to keep highly leveraged products from being sold to customers clearly unable to bear the associated risk.

3. {#q3} What does regulatory oversight of futures markets NOT protect a trader against?
   - [x] Ordinary market risk — losing money on a losing trade
   - Broker misuse of customer funds
   - Exchange rule violations by other members
   - Clearinghouse insolvency in a member default scenario
   > Regulation guards against fraud, misconduct, and counterparty failure — it does nothing to protect against the ordinary risk of a market simply moving against a trader's position.

4. {#q4} In the U.S., which government body regulates futures exchanges and clearinghouses?
   - [x] The Commodity Futures Trading Commission
   - The Federal Reserve
   - The exchange's own board of directors
   - There is no government regulator for U.S. futures markets
   > The CFTC is the U.S. regulator overseeing futures exchanges, clearinghouses, and broker conduct — other countries have their own equivalent regulators performing a similar layered role.

5. {#q5} Why is the clearinghouse's guarantee itself considered a form of trader protection?
   - [x] Because it replaces a trader's exposure to a specific, unknown counterparty with exposure to a well-capitalized, margined, guarantee-fund-backed institution
   - Because the clearinghouse eliminates all market risk for every trader
   - Because clearinghouses are government regulators
   - Because the clearinghouse guarantees a minimum profit on every trade
   > The clearinghouse's novation and margining structure is precisely what converts individual counterparty risk into exposure to a much safer, mutualized guarantee.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A position limit is 6,000 contracts and a trader holds 4,500. How many more contracts can the trader add, and what share of the limit is already used?
   - 1,500, and 25%
   - 4,500, and 75%
   - 6,000, and 100%
   - [x] 1,500, and 75%
   > 6,000 − 4,500 = 1,500 contracts of room, and 4,500 / 6,000 = 75% of the limit is used.

7. {#bk1} [calc] An exchange position limit is 10,000 contracts and a trader holds 7,200. How many more contracts can be added?
   - 10,000
   - 7,200
   - 17,200
   - [x] 2,800
   > 10,000 − 7,200 = 2,800.

8. {#bk2} [calc] A trader holds 7,500 contracts and wants to add 40% more. The position limit is 10,000. By how much would the trader exceed the limit?
   - 0 contracts
   - [x] 500 contracts
   - 3,000 contracts
   - 2,500 contracts
   > A 40% addition is 3,000 contracts, for 10,500 in total. That is 500 above the limit.

9. {#bk3} [calc] A position limit is 10,000 contracts and open interest is 400,000. What fraction of open interest can one trader hold?
   - 25%
   - 0.25%
   - 4%
   - [x] 2.5%
   > 10,000 / 400,000 = 2.5%.

10. {#bk4} What does a national derivatives regulator such as the CFTC do?
   - [x] Oversees exchanges and clearinghouses, sets rules for handling customer funds, and investigates fraud and manipulation
   - Sets the daily settlement price of every contract
   - Guarantees every trader against trading losses
   - Matches buyers and sellers on exchange floors
   > The regulator sits above the exchange and clearinghouse, protecting customers against fraud and misuse of funds.

11. {#bk5} What does an exchange do as a self-regulatory organization?
   - It only provides a trading screen with no rulebook
   - It guarantees traders against price risk
   - [x] It writes and enforces its own rules, including contract specifications, position limits and discipline of members
   - It sets national tax rates on futures gains
   > Exchanges are the front-line regulator of their own markets.

12. {#bk6} Which risk does the regulatory structure NOT remove for a futures trader?
   - [x] Ordinary market risk from price movements and leverage
   - Fraud by a broker
   - Default by another trader
   - Unsuitable product sales
   > Regulation protects against fraud, counterparty default and unsuitable sales. It cannot stop prices from moving against a leveraged position.
