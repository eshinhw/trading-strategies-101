# Quiz

1. {#q1} What are the three core economic functions a futures market serves?
   - [x] Transferring risk (hedging), absorbing risk (speculation), and producing a public price (price discovery)
   - Guaranteeing profits, eliminating volatility, and setting government policy
   - Only allowing physical delivery of commodities
   - Replacing the stock market entirely
   > Hedging, speculation, and price discovery are the three interlocking jobs a futures market performs — each depends on the other two to work well.

2. {#q2} Why do hedgers need speculators?
   - They don't — hedgers could function fine trading only with each other
   - [x] Speculators are willing to take on the price risk hedgers want to shed, giving hedgers someone to actually trade with
   - Speculators set the exchange's daily price limits
   - Speculators are legally required to trade opposite every hedger
   > Without speculators willing to absorb the risk hedgers want to offload, hedgers would be stuck mostly looking for other hedgers with the exact opposite need — a much thinner, harder-to-fill market.

3. {#q3} What is price discovery?
   - [x] The process by which an exchange's continuous, transparent trading produces a real-time, public consensus price
   - A rule requiring all trades to happen at the same price
   - The process of physically inspecting a commodity before delivery
   - A tax authorities use to value futures gains
   > Price discovery is the public, continuously updated price that emerges from open trading — used far beyond the market itself, by anyone who needs a current estimate of an asset's future value.

4. {#q4} Why does liquidity matter to all three of a futures market's core functions?
   - It doesn't — liquidity is unrelated to hedging, speculation, or price discovery
   - [x] Because a thin market makes it hard for hedgers to trade at a fair price, and a price formed in a thin market is less trustworthy as a public benchmark
   - Because liquidity only matters for stock markets, not futures
   - Because illiquid markets always have better price discovery
   > Depth from active speculative trading is what lets hedgers transact efficiently and what makes the resulting price a credible, widely-relied-upon benchmark.

5. {#q5} In the grain elevator example, why does the elevator price its cash wheat off the futures market?
   - [x] Because the futures price is the most liquid, current public estimate of wheat's value, reflecting hedging, speculation, and price discovery all at once
   - Because the exchange legally sets every local cash price
   - Because futures prices are always higher than cash prices
   - Because the elevator is required to trade futures itself
   > The elevator leans on the futures market's public price precisely because it's the best available real-time estimate, produced by the combined activity of hedgers, speculators, and liquidity providers.

6. {#calc1} [calc] Wheat futures are $6.20 and the local elevator pays the futures price less $0.30. How much does a farmer receive for 25,000 bushels?
   - $155,000
   - $7,500
   - [x] $147,500
   - $162,500
   > The cash price is $6.20 − $0.30 = $5.90, and $5.90 × 25,000 = $147,500.

7. {#bk1} [calc] Corn futures are $5.40 and the local elevator pays the futures price less $0.25. What does a farmer receive for 40,000 bushels?
   - $216,000
   - $10,000
   - $5.15
   - [x] $206,000
   > The cash price is $5.40 − $0.25 = $5.15. $5.15 × 40,000 = $206,000.

8. {#bk2} [calc] A miller buys corn futures at $6.00 to hedge 10,000 bushels. At delivery the miller buys at the cash price of $6.50 and sells the futures at $6.45. What is the miller's net cost?
   - $65,000
   - [x] $60,500
   - $64,500
   - $60,000
   > The futures gain is $0.45 per bushel. Net cost per bushel = $6.50 − $0.45 = $6.05. $6.05 × 10,000 = $60,500.

9. {#bk3} [calc] A hedger sells 2 wheat futures (5,000 bushels each) at $6.20 and a speculator buys them. Wheat rises $0.10. What does the speculator gain, and what does the hedger lose on the futures?
   - $1,000 gain and $0 loss
   - $2,000 gain and $1,000 loss
   - $100 gain and $100 loss
   - [x] $1,000 gain and $1,000 loss
   > Each side's result is $0.10 × 10,000 = $1,000. Futures are zero-sum: the speculator gains what the hedger loses.

10. {#bk4} How does a farmer who never trades futures still benefit from the futures market?
   - [x] By using the public futures price to plan, for example when deciding how much to plant or what a buyer should pay
   - By receiving free insurance from the exchange
   - By being forced to deliver crops at the futures price
   - By avoiding all price risk automatically
   > Futures prices are a public, real-time estimate of future value, used far beyond the traders who generate them.

11. {#bk5} What does a hedger give up in exchange for locking in a price?
   - All of their exposure to the underlying business
   - The right to use the exchange again
   - [x] Some potential upside if the price moves in their favour
   - Any obligation to perform at the contract's expiry
   > Hedging trades uncertainty for certainty. The hedger gets a known price but misses out if the market moves their way.

12. {#bk6} Why is the last futures price considered a good consensus estimate of future value?
   - [x] It comes from continuous, transparent trading that reflects every buyer's and seller's information at once
   - The exchange sets it using a secret formula
   - It is the average of the last year's prices
   - It is whatever price a single large trader chooses
   > Open trading combines all participants' information and expectations into one public price.
