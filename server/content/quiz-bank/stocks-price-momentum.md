# Quiz

1. {#q1} What is the core premise of price-momentum?
   - [x] Stocks that have recently outperformed tend to keep outperforming, at least for a while
   - Stocks always revert to their historical average price
   - Stock prices are entirely unpredictable
   - Only the cheapest stocks by valuation will outperform
   > Momentum bets on the persistence of recent relative performance, not reversal — stocks that have outperformed tend to keep outperforming over the following months.

2. {#q2} A classic momentum strategy ranks stocks by:
   - Their dividend yield only
   - [x] Their trailing return over roughly the past 12 months, often skipping the most recent month
   - Their market capitalization alone
   - The company's most recent quarterly earnings report only
   > A standard momentum signal uses trailing 12-month return, commonly skipping the most recent month, which tends to behave differently (short-term reversal) from the broader momentum trend.

3. {#q3} How does cross-sectional stock momentum differ from trend-following in futures?
   - They are identical in every respect
   - [x] Momentum ranks stocks relative to each other at a point in time, rather than reacting to one instrument's own price path
   - Momentum only works on futures contracts, not stocks
   - Momentum ignores price entirely and only uses volume
   > Cross-sectional momentum is a relative-strength approach — it compares stocks to their peers — while trend-following reacts to a single instrument's own price trend in absolute terms.

4. {#q4} What is a "momentum crash"?
   - A momentum strategy's steady, gradual outperformance over many years
   - [x] A sharp, rapid loss that can occur when recent losers, which the strategy is short, bounce back hard during a market recovery
   - A regulatory halt on momentum trading
   - The natural expiration of a momentum-based futures contract
   > Momentum's biggest tail risk is a sharp reversal where the market's worst recent performers — the names a momentum strategy is short or avoiding — rally hard, producing a severe loss concentrated in a short period.

5. {#q5} Why do momentum strategies typically rebalance their holdings periodically, such as monthly?
   - Regulations require monthly rebalancing for all equity strategies
   - [x] A stock's momentum ranking isn't permanent — leadership rotates as new price trends emerge, so the ranking needs recalculating
   - Rebalancing has no effect on strategy performance
   - To avoid ever holding the same stock twice
   > Since momentum is about which stocks are currently leading or lagging, periodic reranking is needed to rotate out of fading leaders and into newly emerging ones.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A momentum strategy is long $10 million of last year's winners, which gain 3.0% this month, and short $10 million of last year's losers, which gain 1.0%. What is the month's profit?
   - $400,000
   - $100,000
   - $300,000
   - [x] $200,000
   > The long leg earns 3.0% × $10M = $300,000 and the short leg loses 1.0% × $10M = $100,000 because the losers rose, so the profit is $200,000.

7. {#calc2} [calc] A stock is up 48% over 12 months, including a 6% gain in the latest month. A momentum signal that skips the latest month uses what return?
   - 42%
   - [x] About 39.6%
   - 48%
   - About 54%
   > Removing the last month: 1.48 / 1.06 − 1 ≈ 39.6%. Skipping it avoids the short-term reversal that often follows a strong month.

8. {#bk1} [calc] A stock was $50 twelve months ago, $62 one month ago, and is $60 today. What is its 12-1 month momentum return, measured from 12 months ago to one month ago?
   - 20%
   - −3.2%
   - [x] 24%
   - 12%
   > The 12-1 return skips the latest month: $62 / $50 − 1 = 24%.

9. {#bk2} [calc] A momentum strategy buys the best 12-month performer and shorts the worst, in equal dollar amounts. Over the next month the winner gains 2% and the loser gains 3%. What is the strategy's return on the long amount?
   - [x] −1%
   - +5%
   - +1%
   - +2%
   > The long earns +2% and the short loses 3%. The net return is 2% − 3% = −1% on each dollar per side.

10. {#bk3} [calc] A stock closed at $40 twelve months ago, $50 one month ago and $48 today. A momentum rule measures the return from 12 months ago to one month ago, skipping the last month. What is that return?
   - 20%
   - −4%
   - [x] 25%
   - 10%
   > ($50 − $40) / $40 = 25%. The latest month's fall to $48 is skipped.

11. {#bk4} [calc] A momentum portfolio is long the top 3 stocks and short the bottom 3, equally weighted within each side. The longs return +3%, +1% and +5% and the shorts return +2%, 0% and +4% next month. What is the long-short return?
   - +3%
   - [x] +1%
   - +9%
   - −1%
   > Long average = 3%. Short average = 2%. Long-short = 3% − 2% = +1%.

12. {#bk5} How does cross-sectional momentum differ from trend following in an individual instrument?
   - It reacts only to one asset's own price path
   - It buys the worst performers
   - It ignores past returns altogether
   - [x] It ranks assets against each other and holds the relative winners, rather than reacting to one asset's own price path
   > Cross-sectional momentum is a relative-strength approach, not an absolute-price approach.

13. {#bk6} What is a momentum crash?
   - A sudden stop in trading on the exchange
   - [x] A sharp loss when markets reverse and the recent losers, which the strategy is short, rebound the hardest
   - A period when all stocks fall equally
   - A loss caused only by high trading fees
   > After a market crash, the stocks that fell the most often bounce back the most, which hurts a momentum strategy short those losers.
