# Quiz

1. {#q1} What is a "support" level, in this context?
   - [x] A price level where a stock's decline has repeatedly stalled in the past, due to historical buying pressure
   - A level that guarantees a stock's price will never fall below it
   - The company's total market capitalization
   - A regulatory price floor set by an exchange
   > Support is identified from a stock's own price history as a level where declines have repeatedly stopped, historically due to buying pressure emerging at that price.

2. {#q2} What does a "bounce" strategy around support and resistance bet on?
   - That support and resistance levels will always break immediately
   - [x] That the level will continue to hold, as it has in the past — buying near support, shorting near resistance
   - That the stock's price will never change again
   - That the company will announce a stock split
   > A bounce strategy bets on the historical pattern repeating — the stock failing to break through the level again and reversing back, just as it has before.

3. {#q3} How does a breakout strategy differ from a bounce strategy at support or resistance?
   - They are the same strategy under different names
   - [x] A breakout strategy bets that when the level finally does break decisively, the move often continues, rather than betting the level holds
   - Breakout strategies never involve support or resistance levels
   - A breakout strategy only works on Fridays
   > Rather than betting on the level holding, a breakout strategy bets that a decisive break through a well-tested level signals a genuine shift in buyer/seller balance likely to continue.

4. {#q4} Why might a level breaking after multiple failed attempts be seen as a meaningful signal?
   - It never signals anything meaningful
   - [x] It can indicate a genuine shift in the underlying balance between buyers and sellers, rather than just another temporary test
   - It always means the company is about to be acquired
   - It guarantees the stock will return to that level immediately
   > A level that has repeatedly held but finally breaks suggests something has changed in the supply/demand balance strongly enough to overcome the historical pattern, which breakout traders interpret as significant.

5. {#q5} What is a core risk of trading support and resistance levels?
   - These levels are perfectly objective and identical for every trader
   - [x] The levels are somewhat subjective and self-referential, so different traders may draw them differently, and their significance can fade over time
   - Support and resistance levels never change once identified
   - There is no risk once a support or resistance level is identified
   > Because support and resistance levels are drawn from historical price action rather than a fixed rule, they carry some subjectivity, and trading a stale or imprecise level — or being wrong about a breakout — is how the approach can lose.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A trader buys at $49 near support at $48 with a stop at $47.50 and a target at $54 near resistance. What is the reward-to-risk ratio?
   - [x] About 3.3 to 1
   - About 0.3 to 1
   - About 1.1 to 1
   - About 10 to 1
   > The risk is $49 − $47.50 = $1.50 and the reward is $54 − $49 = $5.00, so reward / risk = 5.00 / 1.50 ≈ 3.3.

7. {#bk1} [calc] Yesterday a stock had a high of 105, low of 95 and close of 100. What is the pivot point, and what are the first resistance and support levels (R1 = 2P − low, S1 = 2P − high)?
   - P = 100, R1 = 95, S1 = 105
   - P = 105, R1 = 110, S1 = 100
   - P = 100, R1 = 110, S1 = 90
   - [x] P = 100, R1 = 105, S1 = 95
   > P = (105 + 95 + 100) / 3 = 100. R1 = 2 × 100 − 95 = 105. S1 = 2 × 100 − 105 = 95.

   - 50.00
   - [x] 50.33
   - 51.00
   - 49.00
   > P = (52 + 48 + 51) / 3 = 50.33.

9. {#bk3} [calc] A trader buys a breakout at 106 above resistance of 105, places a stop at 103, and targets 112. What is the reward-to-risk ratio?
   - 1 to 2
   - 1 to 1
   - 3 to 1
   - [x] 2 to 1
   > Reward = 112 − 106 = 6. Risk = 106 − 103 = 3. The ratio is 6 / 3 = 2 to 1.

10. {#bk4} [calc] A stock bounces at support of $40 three times and a trader buys at $40.50 with a stop at $39. What is the maximum loss on 400 shares?
   - [x] $600
   - $400
   - $1,500
   - $60
   > Loss per share = $40.50 − $39 = $1.50. $1.50 × 400 = $600.

11. {#bk5} [calc] A stock fails at resistance of $75 and a trader shorts at $74.50, targeting $70 and stopping at $76. What is the reward-to-risk ratio?
   - 1 to 3
   - 4.5 to 1
   - [x] 3 to 1
   - 1 to 1
   > Reward = $74.50 − $70 = $4.50. Risk = $76 − $74.50 = $1.50. 4.50 / 1.50 = 3.
