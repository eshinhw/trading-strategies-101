# Quiz

1. {#q1} What is the purpose of a trade confirmation in a forward contract?
   - [x] It records exactly what the two parties agreed to, serving as the definitive record since there's no exchange trade ticket
   - It cancels the contract if either party disagrees with its terms
   - It replaces the need for any prior negotiation of terms
   - Trade confirmations are only used for exchange-traded futures, never OTC forwards
   > Without an exchange's own trade record, the confirmation is what definitively establishes what both parties actually agreed to.

2. {#q2} What does an ISDA master agreement accomplish?
   - [x] It covers the standard legal terms for all future OTC trades between two counterparties, so each new trade only needs a short confirmation
   - It replaces the need for any trade confirmations
   - It is required by law before any two parties can ever transact
   - It only applies to exchange-traded futures contracts
   > Signing one master agreement upfront avoids renegotiating full legal terms for every individual forward, letting subsequent trades be documented with a brief confirmation instead.

3. {#q3} Why is operational risk from settlement instructions a particular concern for OTC forwards?
   - [x] Because settlement happens bilaterally, with each party's back office independently tracking and reconciling payment details rather than relying on centralized exchange settlement
   - Operational risk does not exist for OTC forwards
   - Because forwards are always settled instantly with no reconciliation needed
   - Because only futures contracts require settlement instructions
   > Without a centralized exchange settlement process, each side of an OTC forward has to independently manage the operational steps that make sure the agreed terms actually settle correctly.

4. {#q4} What changed for OTC derivatives, including forwards, following post-2008 financial crisis reforms?
   - [x] Many jurisdictions now require reporting to trade repositories and, for certain counterparties, regulatory-mandated collateral
   - Forwards became fully exchange-cleared, just like futures
   - OTC derivatives became entirely unregulated
   - Forward contracts were banned in most jurisdictions
   > Post-crisis reforms introduced reporting requirements and collateral rules for OTC derivatives, adding transparency and some clearinghouse-like discipline without centrally clearing forwards themselves.

5. {#q5} Why might a company concentrate its forward trading with a small number of bank counterparties?
   - [x] Because negotiating an ISDA master agreement with a new counterparty can take weeks, making it more efficient to trade repeatedly with banks it's already documented with
   - Because regulators require companies to use only one bank
   - Because trade confirmations are only valid with a single counterparty
   - Because forwards can only legally be traded with one bank per company
   > Once the upfront legal documentation is in place with a given bank, every subsequent trade is much faster to execute — a real incentive to keep trading with already-documented counterparties.

6. {#calc1} [calc] Two banks have forwards worth $6 million in one bank's favor and $2 million in the other's, under an ISDA netting agreement and a CSA with a $1 million threshold. How much collateral is posted?
   - $4 million
   - $5 million
   - $8 million
   - [x] $3 million
   > The net exposure is $6M − $2M = $4M, and collateral covers the amount above the $1M threshold, so $3M.

7. {#bk1} [calc] A forward agreement requires collateral for exposure above a $500,000 threshold. The forward's mark-to-market is $1,200,000 in favour of one party. How much collateral must the other side post?
   - $1,200,000
   - $500,000
   - $1,700,000
   - [x] $700,000
   > Collateral covers the exposure above the threshold: $1,200,000 − $500,000 = $700,000.

8. {#bk2} [calc] A trade confirmation shows 25,000 bushels of wheat at a forward price of $6.45, physically settled. What payment is made at settlement?
   - $6.45
   - [x] $161,250
   - $16,125
   - $1,612,500
   > Payment = 25,000 × $6.45 = $161,250.

9. {#bk3} [calc] Collateral is exchanged as a forward's value changes. The mark-to-market in a party's favour rises from $1.2 million to $1.9 million. How much additional collateral does the other side owe (ignoring thresholds)?
   - $1,900,000
   - $3,100,000
   - $70,000
   - [x] $700,000
   > $1,900,000 − $1,200,000 = $700,000.

10. {#bk4} [calc] A cash-settled currency forward has a notional of €5,000,000 at 1.0700. At maturity the spot rate is 1.0850 and the buyer is long euros. How much does the buyer receive?
   - [x] $75,000
   - $7,500
   - $750,000
   - $5,425,000
   > (1.0850 − 1.0700) × €5,000,000 = $75,000.

11. {#bk5} What is the purpose of a trade confirmation?
   - It guarantees the counterparty will pay
   - It sets the exchange's daily price limit
   - [x] It records the exact agreed terms and is the definitive record of the deal
   - It replaces the need for a forward price
   > With no exchange trade ticket to fall back on, the written confirmation shows the asset, notional amount, delivery date, forward price and settlement method.

12. {#bk6} What does an ISDA Master Agreement do?
   - [x] Covers the standard legal terms for all future OTC trades between two repeat counterparties
   - Replaces the clearinghouse
   - Fixes the forward price of every trade
   - Sets the interest rate on each deal
   > Counterparties sign one master agreement and then only need a short confirmation for each trade.

13. {#bk7} What did post-2008 reforms require for many OTC derivatives, including forwards?
   - Moving all trades onto exchanges
   - A ban on cash settlement
   - [x] Reporting to trade repositories and regulatory collateral for certain counterparties
   - Fixed prices set by regulators
   > The reforms extended some of the transparency and collateral features of exchange trading to the OTC market.
