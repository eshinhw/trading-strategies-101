# Quiz

1. {#q1} What is the core premise behind mean-reversion (contrarian) trading?
   - A price that has moved far from a reference level will keep moving in that same direction
   - [x] A price that has moved unusually far from a reference level will tend to snap back toward it
   - Prices move in a perfectly random walk with no exploitable pattern
   - You should always buy the most expensive asset available
   > Mean-reversion bets on a snapback toward a typical range or average — the opposite premise from trend following's bet on persistence.

2. {#q2} A simple mean-reversion rule might be to buy when price falls:
   - Above its long-term average
   - [x] More than a set number of standard deviations below its short-term average, expecting a bounce back
   - To exactly zero
   - In line with its historical average, with no deviation
   > Buying after an unusually large drop below a short-term average, and expecting reversion toward that average, is a textbook mean-reversion entry signal.

3. {#q3} Compared to trend following, mean-reversion strategies typically show:
   - A low win rate with a few very large winners
   - [x] A high win rate with small, frequent gains, punctuated by occasional large losses
   - An identical risk and reward profile
   - Guaranteed profitability with no downside
   > Most short-term overshoots do revert as expected, producing frequent small wins, but the rare case of a genuine sustained trend can produce an outsized loss for a contrarian position — roughly the mirror image of trend following's profile.

4. {#q4} Why is disciplined risk management, like a hard stop-loss, especially important for mean-reversion trading?
   - It isn't — mean-reversion trades never lose money
   - [x] A real, sustained trend that never reverts can produce an outsized loss if the position isn't cut
   - Stop-losses are required by exchange rules for all futures trades
   - It only matters for trend-following strategies, not contrarian ones
   > Because a contrarian position bets against the current move, the rare case where that move is a genuine new trend, not an overshoot, can run against the position for a long time without risk controls in place.

5. {#q5} A contrarian trader who sells (shorts) after a sharp, unusually large rally is betting that:
   - The rally will continue indefinitely
   - [x] The price is likely to pull back toward a more typical level
   - The asset will be delisted from trading
   - Interest rates will rise
   > The core contrarian bet is that an extreme, fast move is more likely to partially reverse than to keep extending in the same direction.

6. {#q6} [calc] Gold's 20-day average is $1,950 with a $15 standard deviation. What's the two-standard-deviation lower band that would trigger a mean-reversion buy signal?
   - $1,935
   - [x] $1,920
   - $1,905
   - $1,950
   > $1,950 − (2 × $15) = $1,920 — the price level two standard deviations below the 20-day average.

7. {#bk1} [calc] A futures price is 82, its 20-day average is 75, and the standard deviation is 3.5. What is the z-score?
   - 7.0
   - 0.5
   - [x] 2.0
   - 2.3
   > z = (price − average) / standard deviation = (82 − 75) / 3.5 = 2.0.

8. {#bk2} [calc] A contrarian trader sells one contract (1,000 units per contract) at 82, expecting a return to the 75 average. What is the profit if the price gets there?
   - [x] $7,000
   - $3,500
   - $82,000
   - $70,000
   > Profit = (82 − 75) × 1,000 = $7,000.

9. {#bk3} [calc] A contrarian trader uses bands at the 20-day average plus or minus 2 standard deviations. The average is 75 and the standard deviation is 3.5. At what price does a sell signal appear?
   - Above 78.5
   - Above 85.5
   - [x] Above 82
   - Above 75
   > The upper band is 75 + 2 × 3.5 = 82, so prices above 82 trigger a short.
