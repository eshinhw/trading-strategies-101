# Quiz

1. {#q1} What does the VIX index measure?
   - [x] The market's expectation of S&P 500 volatility over the next 30 days, derived from S&P 500 index option prices
   - The S&P 500's actual historical return over the past year
   - The dividend yield of S&P 500 companies
   - The total trading volume of the stock market
   > The VIX is a forward-looking, options-derived measure of expected volatility over the next 30 days, not a backward-looking or unrelated statistic.

2. {#q2} Why is the VIX nicknamed the "fear gauge"?
   - [x] It tends to spike sharply during market selloffs as demand for downside protection rises
   - It only moves when the government makes policy announcements
   - It measures investor happiness during bull markets
   - It has no relationship to actual market conditions
   > Rising demand for protective puts during selloffs pushes up the option prices the VIX is calculated from, causing the VIX to spike during periods of market anxiety.

3. {#q3} How does the VIX typically relate to the S&P 500's own movement?
   - [x] They have a strong negative correlation — the VIX tends to rise when stocks fall
   - They always move in exactly the same direction
   - There is no relationship between the two at all
   - The VIX only moves after the S&P 500 has been flat for a month
   > The VIX's well-documented inverse relationship with the S&P 500 is central to why VIX-linked instruments are used as a potential portfolio hedge.

4. {#q4} Can an investor directly buy or sell "the VIX" itself?
   - [x] No — the VIX is a calculated index, not a tradable security; exposure requires derivatives built on top of it
   - Yes, the VIX trades directly on the NYSE like a stock
   - Yes, but only during the first hour of trading each day
   - The VIX can only be purchased by government entities
   > The VIX itself is just a calculated number — actually trading exposure to it requires VIX futures, options, or exchange-traded products built on those derivatives.

5. {#q5} What tends to happen to the VIX during calm, steadily rising markets?
   - [x] It tends to sit at low, quiet levels
   - It always spikes to record highs
   - It becomes completely unpredictable
   - It stops being calculated entirely
   > With low demand for downside protection during calm markets, the option prices feeding into the VIX stay subdued, keeping the index itself low.

6. {#calc1} [calc] The VIX is 20 and the S&P 500 is at 4,500. About how many index points is the expected one-month move (one standard deviation)?
   - About 900 points
   - [x] About 260 points
   - About 225 points
   - About 1,300 points
   > The monthly move is 20% / √12 ≈ 5.8%, and 5.8% × 4,500 ≈ 260 points.

7. {#calc2} [calc] The VIX is 25 and the S&P 500 is at 5,000. About how many index points is the expected one-day move (one standard deviation)?
   - About 250 points
   - About 1.6 points
   - About 125 points
   - [x] About 79 points
   > The daily volatility is 25% / √252 ≈ 1.575%, and 1.575% × 5,000 ≈ 79 points.

8. {#bk1} [calc] The VIX closes at 24. What one-standard-deviation move in the S&P 500 does that imply over the next month?
   - [x] 6.93%
   - 1.51%
   - 2.00%
   - 24.00%
   > The VIX is an annualized volatility. For one month, divide by √12: 24% / 3.464 = 6.93%.

9. {#bk2} [calc] With the S&P 500 at 4,500 and the VIX at 18, what is the approximate one-standard-deviation range for the next month, in index points?
   - ±810 points
   - ±51 points
   - [x] ±234 points
   - ±468 points
   > One-month move = 4,500 × 18% / √12 = 234 points.
