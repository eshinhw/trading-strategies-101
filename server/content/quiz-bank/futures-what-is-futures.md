# Quiz

1. {#q1} In the simplest terms, what is a futures contract?
   - [x] An agreement to buy or sell an asset at an agreed price on an agreed future date, the same basic idea as a forward
   - A contract that guarantees a fixed profit
   - A type of loan with no fixed repayment date
   - An agreement that only applies to stocks, never commodities
   > A futures contract promises exactly the same basic thing a forward does — buy or sell at an agreed price on an agreed future date.

2. {#q2} What fundamentally distinguishes a futures contract from a forward contract?
   - Futures promise something completely different from what a forward promises
   - [x] How the agreement is made and enforced — traded on an organized exchange rather than negotiated privately between two parties
   - Only futures contracts involve a long and a short side
   - Forwards can never be used for commodities like oil
   > The underlying promise is identical to a forward's — what changes is that a futures contract is exchange-traded and standardized, rather than a private, bilateral agreement.

3. {#q3} Why can a trader go long a crude oil futures contract without finding a specific seller willing to make a private deal?
   - [x] Because the futures contract is bought and sold on an organized exchange, where any trader can take either side of the trade
   - Because futures contracts don't actually require a counterparty at all
   - Because oil futures are the only type of futures contract that exists
   - Because the trader is required to physically own oil first
   > The exchange, not a specific counterparty, is what makes it possible to go long or short a futures contract instantly, without negotiating a private deal the way a forward would require.

4. {#q4} Which forward-contract concepts carry over directly to futures?
   - Nothing carries over — futures and forwards are entirely unrelated
   - [x] Long and short positions, and the idea of agreeing on a price today for a transaction later
   - Only the idea of daily margin calls
   - Only the concept of a clearinghouse
   > The core long/short, price-agreed-today-for-later logic is shared between forwards and futures — what sets futures apart is margin, standardization, and the clearinghouse.

5. {#q5} Beyond the basic agree-a-price-now, transact-later idea, what is specifically different about futures compared with forwards?
   - [x] What's specifically different about futures — margin and daily settlement, standardized contract terms, and the clearinghouse
   - Nothing — futures and forwards work identically in every respect
   - Only how to physically deliver a commodity
   - Only the fact that futures can be traded online
   > Futures share the forward's core logic, but add the mechanics that actually distinguish them — margin, standardization, and the clearinghouse guarantee.

6. {#q6} How does a futures contract differ from an option, in terms of obligation?
   - [x] An option buyer pays a premium for the right, not the obligation, to transact — a futures long and short are both obligated to settle (or close out) the position
   - They are identical — both sides can always walk away for free
   - Only the futures short has any obligation at all
   - Options and futures never share any long/short structure
   > An option's buyer has optionality — they can let it expire worthless — while both sides of a futures contract carry a real, symmetric obligation, which is exactly why futures need daily margining.

7. {#q7} [calc] Two traders take opposite sides of an E-mini S&P 500 contract ($50 multiplier) at 4,500.00, which settles the next day at 4,510.00. What happens?
   - [x] The long is credited $500 and the short is debited $500
   - Both traders are credited $500
   - Neither trader's account changes until expiration
   - The short is credited $500 and the long is debited $500
   > A 10-point gain × the $50 multiplier is $500: the long, who benefits when price rises, is credited that amount, while the short is debited the identical $500 — one side's gain is exactly the other's loss.

8. {#bk1} [calc] A trader buys 6 E-mini S&P 500 contracts (multiplier $50) at 4,200, and the price later moves to 4,236. What is the profit or loss on the position?
   - +$219
   - +$1,825
   - −$10,950
   - [x] +$10,950
   > The move is 4,236 − 4,200 = 36.50 per unit, and 36.50 × 50 × 6 contracts = $10,950.

9. {#bk2} [calc] A trader buys 3 corn contracts (5,000 bushels each) at 5.80, and the price later moves to 6.15. What is the profit or loss on the position?
   - +$1.05
   - [x] +$5,250
   - +$1,750
   - −$5,250
   > The move is 6.15 − 5.80 = 0.35 per unit, and 0.35 × 5,000 × 3 contracts = $5,250.

10. {#bk3} [calc] A trader sells 3 gold futures (100 ounces each) at $2,010. The price falls to $1,985. What is the profit?
   - $750
   - $75,000
   - −$7,500
   - [x] $7,500
   > A short gains when the price falls: ($2,010 − $1,985) × 100 × 3 = $7,500.

11. {#bk4} [calc] A trader buys 2 E-mini S&P 500 futures (multiplier $50) at 4,350. The index falls to 4,310. What is the result?
   - [x] −$4,000
   - −$80
   - +$4,000
   - −$40,000
   > The move is −40 points. −40 × $50 × 2 = −$4,000.

12. {#bk5} [calc] A trader buys one crude oil future (1,000 barrels) at $80 and another trader buys a $80 call option for $3 per barrel. Oil rises to $86. How much more does the futures position earn than the option, in total dollars?
   - $6,000
   - $9,000
   - [x] $3,000
   - $0
   > The future earns $6 × 1,000 = $6,000. The call earns ($6 − $3) × 1,000 = $3,000. The difference is $3,000.

13. {#bk6} [calc] A trader buys one crude oil future (1,000 barrels) at $80 and another buys an $80 call for $3 per barrel. Oil falls to $74. How much more does the futures position lose than the option?
   - [x] $3,000
   - $6,000
   - $3 per barrel more than the premium
   - $0, because both lose the same
   > The future loses $6 × 1,000 = $6,000. The call loses only its $3,000 premium. The future loses $3,000 more.

14. {#bk7} What does buying a futures contract commit the buyer to?
   - Buying the asset only if the price is favourable on the delivery date
   - Paying a premium for the right to buy later without any obligation
   - [x] Buying the asset at the agreed price on the agreed date, or settling the difference in cash
   - Owning a share of the company that issued the asset
   > A futures buyer is obligated to transact at the agreed price. Unlike an option buyer, the buyer cannot walk away if the market moves against them.

15. {#bk8} How does a futures contract differ from an option?
   - A futures buyer can let the contract expire worthless at no cost
   - An option obligates both sides, while a futures contract obligates neither
   - A futures contract requires a premium that an option does not
   - [x] Both sides of a futures contract are obligated, while an option buyer has a right but no obligation
   > A futures contract binds both parties to the trade. An option buyer pays a premium for a right and can simply let the option expire.

16. {#bk9} What is the main difference between a futures contract and a forward contract?
   - A future is customized between two parties, while a forward is traded on an exchange
   - [x] A future is standardized and traded on an exchange, while a forward is customized and negotiated privately
   - A future has no obligation to buy or sell, while a forward does
   - A future can only be settled in cash, while a forward can only be delivered physically
   > Both promise a transaction at an agreed price later. What differs is how the deal is made and enforced: exchange-traded and standardized for futures, private and customized for forwards.
