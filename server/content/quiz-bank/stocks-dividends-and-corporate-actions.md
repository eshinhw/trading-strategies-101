# Quiz

1. {#q1} What is a dividend?
   - [x] A direct cash payment a company makes to its shareholders, typically out of its profits
   - A mandatory fee shareholders must pay the company
   - A type of stock split
   - A loan shareholders make to the company
   > A dividend is a cash distribution to shareholders, usually paid out of company profits on a regular schedule — not every company pays one.

2. {#q2} What happens to a shareholder's total position value in a stock split?
   - It doubles automatically
   - [x] It stays the same — the share count increases and the price per share decreases proportionally
   - It falls to zero
   - Splits always reduce a shareholder's total position value
   > A split changes the share count and per-share price by offsetting amounts, so the total value of a shareholder's position is unaffected.

3. {#q3} How does a share buyback affect remaining shareholders?
   - [x] It increases each remaining shareholder's proportional ownership of the company, without a cash dividend payout
   - It dilutes every shareholder's ownership stake
   - It has no effect on shareholder ownership at all
   - It automatically triggers a stock split
   > Retiring shares through a buyback reduces the total share count, which increases each remaining shareholder's proportional slice of the company.

4. {#q4} Why do younger, fast-growing companies often choose not to pay dividends?
   - They are legally prohibited from paying dividends
   - [x] They typically reinvest profits back into the business instead, favoring share-price growth over a cash payout
   - Dividends are only available to private companies
   - Fast-growing companies never generate any profit
   > Reinvesting profits to fuel further growth is a common alternative to paying a dividend, especially for companies still in a high-growth phase.

5. {#q5} Why are quarterly earnings reports closely watched by the market?
   - [x] They're often the single biggest source of new information used to reassess what a company is actually worth
   - They have no effect on a stock's price
   - They are only relevant to company employees
   - Earnings reports are published just once every ten years
   > Quarterly earnings reports deliver fresh, concrete information about a company's actual performance, which is exactly the kind of news that moves the market's view of what the stock is worth.

6. {#calc1} [calc] An investor holds 90 shares at $150. The company does a 3-for-1 split. What is the holding afterwards?
   - [x] 270 shares at $50, still worth $13,500
   - 270 shares at $150, worth $40,500
   - 30 shares at $450, worth $13,500
   - 270 shares at $50, worth $4,500
   > Shares triple (90 × 3 = 270) and the price falls to a third ($50). The value is unchanged: 270 × $50 = $13,500.

7. {#bk1} [calc] A stock trades at $50 and pays $1.60 in dividends a year. What is its dividend yield?
   - [x] 3.20%
   - 6.40%
   - 1.60%
   - 1.65%
   > Dividend yield = annual dividend / price = $1.60 / $50 = 3.20%.

   > Dividend yield = annual dividend / price = $3.75 / $125 = 3.00%.

10. {#bk4} [calc] An investor owns 500 shares at $120 before a 3-for-1 stock split. After the split, how many shares are held and at what approximate price?
   - 166 shares at $360
   - 1,500 shares at $120
   - 500 shares at $40
   - [x] 1,500 shares at $40
   > A 3-for-1 split multiplies shares by 3 and divides the price by 3: 500 × 3 = 1,500 shares, $120 / 3 = $40. The total value is unchanged at $60,000.

11. {#bk5} [calc] An investor owns 1,000 shares at $90 before a 2-for-1 stock split. After the split, how many shares are held and at what approximate price?
   - 500 shares at $180
   - [x] 2,000 shares at $45
   - 2,000 shares at $90
   - 1,000 shares at $45
   > A 2-for-1 split multiplies shares by 2 and divides the price by 2: 1,000 × 2 = 2,000 shares, $90 / 2 = $45. The total value is unchanged at $90,000.

12. {#bk6} [calc] A stock closes at $60.00 the day before it goes ex-dividend with a $1.20 dividend. Ignoring other price moves, what is a reasonable opening price on the ex-dividend date?
   - $61.20
   - $60.00
   - $59.40
   - [x] $58.80
   > On the ex-dividend date the share price drops by about the dividend: $60.00 − $1.20 = $58.80.

13. {#bk7} [calc] A company earns $3.00 per share and pays $1.20 per share in dividends. What is the payout ratio?
   - 60%
   - [x] 40%
   - 2.5%
   - 160%
   > Payout ratio = dividends / earnings = $1.20 / $3.00 = 40%.

14. {#bk8} [calc] A company earns $500 million with 100 million shares outstanding. It buys back and retires 10 million shares. If net income is unchanged, what is the new earnings per share?
   - [x] $5.56
   - $5.00
   - $4.55
   - $6.00
   > Before: $500 million / 100 million = $5.00. After: $500 million / 90 million = $5.56.

15. {#bk9} [calc] An investor owns 1,000 shares at $2.00 when the company does a 1-for-5 reverse split. How many shares and at what approximate price after the split?
   - 5,000 shares at $0.40
   - 200 shares at $2.00
   - [x] 200 shares at $10.00
   - 1,000 shares at $10.00
   > A 1-for-5 reverse split divides the share count by 5 and multiplies the price by 5. Total value stays $2,000.

16. {#bk10} [calc] An investor receives a $480 dividend and reinvests it all at $48 per share. How many shares are bought?
   - [x] 10
   - 100
   - 48
   - 0.1
   > $480 / $48 = 10 shares.

17. {#bk11} What happens to the total value a shareholder holds in a stock split?
   - It doubles with a two-for-one split
   - It falls, because the price per share falls
   - [x] It does not change, because the share count rises while the price per share falls in proportion
   - It rises, because the number of shares rises
   > A split changes the number of shares and the price per share by the same ratio, so what a shareholder owns is worth the same.

18. {#bk12} What does a share buyback do to each remaining shareholder's ownership?
   - [x] It increases each remaining shareholder's proportional ownership, because shares are retired
   - It reduces ownership, because the company issues new shares
   - It leaves ownership unchanged and pays cash dividends
   - It transfers shares to the company's board
   > The company uses cash to retire shares, so the same company is divided into fewer shares.

19. {#bk13} Why do many young, fast-growing companies pay no dividend?
   - They are not allowed by law to pay dividends
   - Dividends are only paid by bond issuers
   - [x] They reinvest all their profits in growth
   - They do not have shareholders
   > A company can retain its profits to fund expansion instead of paying them out.
