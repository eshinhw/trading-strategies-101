# Quiz

1. {#q1} What kind of uncertainty does a forward contract mainly address?
   - Uncertainty about whether a transaction will happen at all
   - [x] Uncertainty about the price or exchange rate of a transaction a business knows it will make later
   - Uncertainty about the weather
   - Uncertainty about which exchange to use
   > A forward helps when quantity and timing are known but the future price is not, by fixing the price today.

2. {#q2} What does each party give up when it enters a forward?
   - Nothing, since both sides always benefit
   - [x] The chance to benefit if the market moves in its favor, in exchange for certainty about the price
   - The right to ever trade the asset again
   - Ownership of its business
   > Whichever way the price moves, one party would have done better by waiting, and both accept that in return for a known price.

3. {#q3} Why are forwards still used even though standardized futures exist?
   - Futures are illegal for businesses
   - Forwards are always cheaper
   - [x] A forward can be tailored to the exact quantity, date, quality, and location of the exposure being hedged
   - Forwards have no risk
   > Because a forward is negotiated privately, it can match a specific exposure exactly, which a standardized contract can't.

4. {#q4} What is the main trade-off of that flexibility?
   - Forwards must be traded on an exchange
   - There is no flexibility at all
   - There is no trade-off
   - [x] Each party relies on the other to perform and can't easily exit the contract early
   > A private contract carries counterparty risk and is harder to close out than an exchange-traded contract.

5. {#q5} How did futures and options relate to the forward contract historically?
   - Futures and options came before forwards
   - [x] Futures standardized and exchange-cleared the forward idea, and options added the choice of whether to go through with the deal
   - They are the same instrument
   - Forwards were created to replace options
   > The forward is the simplest derivative, and later instruments were built to address its limits in counterparty risk, exit, and optionality.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A farmer agrees a forward at $6.25 for 8,000 bushels, and the harvest-time price is $5.60. How much better off is the farmer than selling at the market price?
   - [x] $5,200
   - $50,000
   - $44,800
   - $520
   > The forward fixes $6.25 × 8,000 = $50,000, against 8,000 × $5.60 = $44,800 in the market, so the farmer is $5,200 better off.

7. {#bk1} [calc] A cereal maker needs 50,000 bushels of corn in 6 months and fears a price rise. Spot is $5.80 and the forward is $6.10. If spot at delivery is $6.60, how much does the forward save versus buying at spot then?
   - $15,000
   - $40,000
   - [x] $25,000
   - $5,000
   > The forward fixes $6.10 instead of $6.60, saving $0.50 × 50,000 = $25,000.

8. {#bk2} [calc] A bakery agrees to buy 30,000 bushels of wheat forward at $6.60. At delivery the market price is $7.20. How much does the forward save the bakery?
   - [x] $18,000
   - $198,000
   - $1,800
   - $216,000
   > The bakery pays $0.60 less per bushel than the market: $0.60 × 30,000 = $18,000.

9. {#bk3} [calc] A bakery agrees to buy 30,000 bushels of wheat forward at $6.60. At delivery the market price is $6.10. How much more does it pay than it would have at the market?
   - $18,000
   - $198,000
   - [x] $15,000
   - $1,500
   > The bakery pays $0.50 more per bushel: $0.50 × 30,000 = $15,000. That is the price of certainty.

10. {#bk4} [calc] An importer owes €1.5 million in 90 days and locks in a forward rate of 1.0950 dollars per euro. How many dollars will it pay?
   - $1,369,863
   - [x] $1,642,500
   - $1,500,000
   - $16,425
   > €1,500,000 × 1.0950 = $1,642,500.

11. {#bk5} [calc] A farmer expects 5,000 bushels but signs a forward to sell 10,000 bushels at $6.30. At delivery the price is $6.70. What is the loss on the 5,000 bushels the farmer does not own?
   - $4,000
   - $20,000
   - $200
   - [x] $2,000
   > The extra 5,000 bushels are an unhedged short: ($6.70 − $6.30) × 5,000 = $2,000. A tailor-made forward avoids this by matching the exact quantity.

12. {#bk6} What do both sides of a forward get in exchange for giving up the chance to benefit if the market moves in their favour?
   - A guaranteed profit
   - [x] Certainty about the price they will pay or receive
   - Protection from counterparty default
   - The right to cancel at any time
   > Whichever way the market moves, one party would have been better off waiting. Both accept that for a known price.

13. {#bk7} Why does a farmer with 5,000 bushels arriving in October benefit from a tailor-made forward?
   - It makes the farmer's crop sell at a higher price
   - It guarantees that spot prices will not fall
   - It removes the farmer's obligation to deliver
   - [x] It can be sized and dated to the exact exposure, instead of being forced into a standard contract
   > A forward can match the real quantity, date, quality and location, avoiding a mismatch that would leave part of the exposure unhedged or over-hedged.

14. {#bk8} How do forwards relate to other derivatives such as futures?
   - Futures came first and forwards were created later to replace them
   - Forwards and futures are unrelated
   - [x] A forward is the simplest derivative and futures were built on it by standardizing the contract and routing it through an exchange
   - A forward is a futures contract with a premium
   > Agreements to deliver later at a price set today are among the oldest financial contracts. Exchanges later standardized them as futures.
