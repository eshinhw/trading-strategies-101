# Quiz

1. {#q1} What does "carry" refer to in fixed income?
   - The physical transportation of bonds between markets
   - [x] The return an investor earns simply from holding a bond over time if market rates, spreads, and the curve's shape stay unchanged
   - A bond's credit rating
   - The fee charged by a broker to trade a bond
   > Carry is the return that accrues to a bondholder purely from holding the position, assuming no adverse change in the underlying market conditions.

2. {#q2} What are two sources of carry for a bond position?
   - [x] Credit carry (yield relative to the risk-free rate) and term carry (a longer-maturity bond's higher yield on an upward-sloping curve)
   - Only the bond's face value
   - Only changes in a company's stock price
   - The number of coupon payments per year alone
   > Carry can come from a bond's credit spread over the risk-free rate, or from the additional yield a longer maturity offers on an upward-sloping curve, closely related to rolling down the curve.

3. {#q3} How does a systematic carry strategy typically select bonds?
   - By avoiding all bonds with any yield whatsoever
   - [x] By ranking available bonds by carry-per-unit-of-risk and tilting the portfolio toward the highest-ranked ones
   - By selecting bonds at random regardless of yield
   - By only holding bonds with zero carry
   > The strategy explicitly ranks and favors bonds offering the most carry relative to their risk, rather than an unweighted selection.

4. {#q4} What is the central risk of a carry strategy?
   - Carry strategies have no risk once implemented
   - [x] It can suffer sharp losses when the conditions the carry was compensating for actually occur, such as a sudden spread widening or sharp rate rise
   - Carry strategies only work if interest rates are exactly zero
   - The risk that bond issuers stop paying coupons entirely
   > Carry assumes today's yields are a reasonable guide to the future; when the risks embedded in that yield materialize, like a spread blowout, losses can occur faster than accumulated carry can offset.

5. {#q5} What business is a carry strategy's return profile often compared to?
   - A bank's checking account services
   - [x] An insurance-selling business — collecting steady premiums in exchange for bearing the risk of an occasional larger payout
   - A charity with no revenue model
   - A business with no exposure to any form of risk
   > Carry strategies typically earn small, steady gains most of the time, punctuated by occasional larger losses when the underlying risk materializes — a pattern that resembles selling insurance.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A $20 million bond position yields 5.5% and is funded at 4.0%. If nothing else changes, what is the annual carry?
   - $1,100,000
   - $800,000
   - [x] $300,000
   - $30,000
   > The carry is (5.5% − 4.0%) × $20,000,000 = $300,000 a year.

7. {#bk1} [calc] A bond yields 4.8% and the investor's funding cost is 3.0%. What is the carry?
   - 7.8%
   - [x] 1.8%
   - 3.0%
   - 4.8%
   > Carry = yield − funding cost = 4.8% − 3.0% = 1.8%.

8. {#bk2} [calc] A bond yields 4.8%, funding costs 3.0%, and roll-down adds 0.4% a year. What is the total expected return from carry and roll-down?
   - 1.8%
   - 0.4%
   - 8.2%
   - [x] 2.2%
   > Total = (4.8% − 3.0%) + 0.4% = 2.2%.

9. {#bk3} [calc] A carry strategy holds $50 million of bonds yielding 4.8% funded at 3.0%. What is the annual carry in dollars?
   - $2,400,000
   - [x] $900,000
   - $1,500,000
   - $90,000
   > Carry = ($50 million) × (4.8% − 3.0%) = $50 million × 1.8% = $900,000.
