# Quiz

1. {#q1} What is the "bond floor"?
   - [x] The value a convertible trades at based on its coupon and principal repayment, which limits how far its price can fall even if the stock drops
   - The maximum price a convertible can ever reach
   - The stock price at which conversion becomes mandatory
   - A fee charged when converting a bond into shares
   > The bond floor reflects the convertible's value as a straight bond, setting a limit on how far its price falls even during a stock decline.

2. {#q2} What happens to a convertible's price behavior when the stock rises well above the conversion price?
   - [x] It starts trading almost in lockstep with the underlying stock
   - It becomes completely disconnected from the stock price
   - Its price is capped and cannot rise further
   - It converts automatically into a plain bond
   > Once conversion is clearly the better choice, the convertible's value tracks the stock closely, since the bond floor is no longer the binding constraint.

3. {#q3} What is the convertible premium?
   - [x] The amount by which a convertible typically trades above both its bond floor and its conversion value
   - A fee paid to the issuer when purchasing a convertible
   - The difference between two different bonds' coupon rates
   - A penalty for holding a convertible past its maturity date
   > The premium compensates for the flexibility of holding an instrument that captures the better of bond-like safety or stock-like upside without pre-committing to either.

4. {#q4} How does a convertible's price relate to the stock price across the full range of stock prices?
   - [x] It traces a curve — flat and bond-like at low prices, increasingly stock-like at high prices, with the premium most visible in between
   - It's always a straight, one-to-one line matching the stock price exactly
   - It's completely flat and unrelated to the stock price at every level
   - It moves in the opposite direction of the stock price at every level
   > The relationship is curved, not linear, reflecting the shift from bond-dominated to stock-dominated value as the stock price rises.

5. {#q5} A convertible's price curves: it tracks the stock on the way up but flattens toward its bond floor on the way down. What does this give a convertible investor?
   - [x] Participation in a rising stock while cushioning a falling one
   - Guaranteed profit regardless of what the stock does
   - Exposure only to downside risk, with no upside potential
   - The exact same risk profile as owning the stock outright
   > The combination of a bond floor on the downside and stock-like upside participation is the defining appeal of holding a convertible.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A convertible has a conversion ratio of 20 and a bond floor of $850. The stock falls to $30. What is the conversion value, and what sets the convertible's price?
   - [x] Conversion value is $600, so the $850 bond floor sets the price
   - Conversion value is $600, so $600 sets the price
   - Conversion value is $1,500, so it sets the price
   - Conversion value is $50, so the bond floor does not matter
   > Conversion value is 20 × $30 = $600, which is below the $850 floor, so the bond floor is what the price is anchored to.

7. {#calc2} [calc] A convertible trades at $1,150 and converts into 20 shares of a $48 stock. What is its conversion premium over parity?
   - About 15%
   - About 12%
   - [x] About 19.8%
   - About 30%
   > Parity is 20 × $48 = $960. The premium is ($1,150 − $960) / $960 ≈ 19.8%.

8. {#calc3} [calc] A convertible with a conversion ratio of 20 and a bond floor of $900 trades when the stock is $55. What is its conversion value, and does it exceed the floor?
   - [x] $1,100, and yes
   - $1,100, and no
   - $900, and yes
   - $55, and no
   > Conversion value is 20 × $55 = $1,100, which is above the $900 floor, so the stock value is what the price now follows.

9. {#bk1} [calc] A convertible has a bond floor of $920 and a conversion value of $800. Roughly what is the least it should trade for?
   - $800
   - $1,000
   - [x] $920
   - $1,720
   > Investors can hold the bond for its $920 floor value instead of converting into $800 of stock, so the price should not fall much below $920.

10. {#bk2} [calc] A convertible bond has a bond floor of $920 and a conversion value of $1,150. What is the minimum value it should have?
   - [x] $1,150
   - $920
   - $2,070
   - $1,035
   > A convertible is worth at least the greater of its bond floor and its conversion value, so $1,150.

11. {#bk3} [calc] A convertible has a conversion ratio of 20 and the option delta is 0.6. If the stock rises $5, by roughly how much does the bond price rise?
   - $100
   - $5
   - [x] $60
   - $30
   > Equivalent shares = 20 × 0.6 = 12. 12 × $5 = $60.

12. {#bk4} [calc] A convertible's bond floor is $920. Interest rates rise and the floor falls 4%. What is the new bond floor?
   - $916
   - [x] $883.20
   - $880
   - $957
   > $920 × (1 − 0.04) = $883.20.

13. {#bk5} [calc] A convertible trades at $1,150 with a bond floor of $920. How much is the market paying for the conversion option?
   - $1,150
   - $920
   - $2,070
   - [x] $230
   > Option value ≈ price − bond floor = $1,150 − $920 = $230.
