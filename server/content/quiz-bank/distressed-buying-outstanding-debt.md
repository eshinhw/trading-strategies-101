# Quiz

1. {#q1} Where is most distressed debt actually purchased from?
   - [x] The secondary market, often from existing holders who want out of the position
   - Directly from the issuing company at face value
   - Distressed debt cannot legally be bought or sold at all
   - Only from government auctions
   > Secondary-market purchases from existing holders, not direct issuance, are the typical way distressed debt changes hands.

2. {#q2} Why do some sellers of distressed debt sell regardless of price?
   - [x] Mandate, regulatory, or risk-tolerance constraints can force a sale that isn't based on a considered credit judgment
   - All sellers of distressed debt are making a fully considered economic decision
   - There is no such thing as a forced or motivated seller in distressed markets
   - Sellers always hold their position until the company fully recovers
   > Structural constraints unrelated to the credit itself create real, exploitable selling pressure for a buyer without those same constraints.

3. {#q3} Instead of concentrating in one issuer, what does a strategy of buying outstanding distressed debt do?
   - [x] Builds a broad, diversified book of positions across many issuers and capital-structure levels
   - Concentrates all capital in a single company's most junior debt
   - Avoids holding any distressed debt securities whatsoever
   - Buys only equity, never debt, in distressed companies
   > Treating distressed-debt sourcing as a repeatable, diversified, portfolio-level process is the defining feature of this approach.

4. {#q4} How does broadly buying outstanding distressed debt differ from a loan-to-own approach?
   - [x] Loan-to-own deliberately concentrates in one company's debt to pursue control, while broad debt buying is closer to portfolio construction
   - The two strategies are functionally identical
   - Loan-to-own never involves purchasing any actual debt securities
   - Broad debt buying always seeks controlling equity ownership, exactly like loan-to-own
   > The two strategies differ in concentration and intent — one is a diversified sourcing approach, the other a concentrated bid for control.

5. {#q5} Why does a fund that buys distressed debt maintain standing relationships with banks and insurers?
   - [x] Those institutions periodically need to offload distressed positions for reasons unrelated to the credit itself, creating a repeatable sourcing channel
   - The relationships are purely social and have no connection to sourcing debt
   - Banks and insurers are legally required to sell only to this one fund
   - The fund uses these relationships exclusively to sell its own holdings
   > Cultivating relationships with structurally motivated sellers is exactly what lets the fund source distressed debt on a repeatable, diversified basis.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A fund buys 40 distressed positions at 40 cents, $2 million of cost each. Expected recovery is 46 cents. What is the total expected profit?
   - $0.3 million
   - $4.8 million
   - $24 million
   - [x] $12 million
   > Each position has $5M of face value ($2M / 0.40). Recovery is $5M × 0.46 = $2.3M, a $0.3M profit, and 40 × $0.3M = $12M.

7. {#calc2} [calc] Each distressed position's outcome has a standard deviation equal to 50% of its cost. If 25 independent positions of equal size are held, what is the portfolio's standard deviation?
   - 50%
   - [x] 10%
   - 25%
   - 2%
   > Diversifying over independent positions divides the risk by the square root of 25 = 5, so 50% / 5 = 10%.

8. {#bk1} [calc] An investor buys distressed bonds at 40 cents on the dollar, and the bonds later recover 65 cents. What is the return?
   - [x] 62.5%
   - 25%
   - 162.5%
   - 38.5%
   > Return = (65 − 40) / 40 = 62.5%.

9. {#bk2} [calc] A bond with an 8% coupon on a $100 face trades at 50. What is the current yield?
   - 8%
   - 4%
   - [x] 16%
   - 50%
   > Current yield = annual coupon / price = $8 / $50 = 16%, though the coupon may not be paid in distress.

10. {#bk3} [calc] An investor buys $5 million of face value at 45 and later receives 60 cents on the dollar. What is the profit?
   - [x] $750,000
   - $3,000,000
   - $2,250,000
   - $1,000,000
   > Cost = $5 million × 0.45 = $2.25 million. Proceeds = $5 million × 0.60 = $3.0 million. Profit = $750,000.
