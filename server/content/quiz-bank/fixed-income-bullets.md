# Quiz

1. {#q1} What defines a bullet bond portfolio?
   - Holdings spread evenly across every available maturity
   - [x] Holdings concentrated around a single target maturity date
   - A portfolio containing only zero-coupon bonds
   - A portfolio that holds no bonds longer than one year
   > A bullet portfolio clusters its holdings around one specific maturity, producing a sharp spike in the portfolio's maturity distribution rather than a spread.

2. {#q2} Why is a bullet portfolio's duration relatively easy to manage?
   - Duration is irrelevant to bullet portfolios
   - [x] Because every bond matures around the same date, the portfolio's overall interest-rate sensitivity stays fairly stable without much active rebalancing
   - Because bullet portfolios never contain more than one bond
   - Because bullet portfolios are legally required to have zero duration
   > Clustering maturities around one point keeps the portfolio's aggregate duration predictable and stable, since bonds aren't aging at different rates relative to each other across a wide maturity range.

3. {#q3} When is a bullet strategy a natural fit for an investor?
   - When the investor has no specific future cash need
   - [x] When the investor has a specific future liability or cash need at a known date
   - When the investor wants maximum diversification across the yield curve
   - When the investor wants to avoid holding any fixed income at all
   > A bullet portfolio's bonds can be timed to mature right when a known future liability comes due, such as a pension payout, minimizing the need to sell early.

4. {#q4} What is the main tradeoff of concentrating a portfolio in a single maturity?
   - Bullet portfolios cannot hold government bonds
   - [x] Increased exposure to a shift specifically at that point on the yield curve, compared to a portfolio spread across many maturities
   - Bullet portfolios always underperform every other bond strategy
   - There is no tradeoff — concentration is strictly better
   > Because the portfolio's fate depends heavily on rates at one specific maturity, it's more exposed to a "twist" or shift at that point on the curve than a diversified, spread-out portfolio would be.

5. {#q5} How does a bullet portfolio's maturity distribution look on a chart, and why does it get its name from that shape?
   - Flat and even across all maturities — like a barbell
   - [x] A single sharp spike concentrated at one maturity — resembling a bullet
   - Two separate spikes at the short and long ends
   - A smooth curve gradually declining to zero
   > The strategy is named for the shape of its maturity distribution: a tight cluster of holdings around one target date looks like a single spike, or bullet, rather than being spread out.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A $10 million bullet portfolio has a duration of 4.2, and yields rise 0.5 percentage points. About how much does its value fall?
   - About $42,000
   - About $2,100,000
   - [x] About $210,000
   - About $500,000
   > The price falls about 4.2 × 0.5% = 2.1%, and 2.1% × $10,000,000 = $210,000.

7. {#bk1} [calc] A bullet portfolio puts $10 million in 5-year bonds with a modified duration of 4.5. Yields rise 0.5%. What is the approximate loss?
   - $22,500
   - $2,250,000
   - [x] $225,000
   - $45,000
   > Loss ≈ duration × yield change × value = 4.5 × 0.005 × $10 million = $225,000.

8. {#bk2} [calc] A $10 million bullet portfolio of 5-year bonds yields 4.0%. What is the approximate yearly coupon income?
   - [x] $400,000
   - $40,000
   - $4,000,000
   - $200,000
   > Income ≈ 4.0% × $10 million = $400,000.

9. {#bk3} [calc] A bullet in 5-year bonds has a duration of 4.5. A barbell uses 2-year bonds (duration 1.9) and 10-year bonds (duration 8.0). What share in the 2-year bond gives the barbell the same duration as the bullet?
   - 50%
   - 42.6%
   - [x] 57.4%
   - 65%
   > Solve w × 1.9 + (1 − w) × 8.0 = 4.5, so w = (8.0 − 4.5) / (8.0 − 1.9) = 3.5 / 6.1 = 57.4%.
