## The Problem: Trusting Middlemen

Online payments normally rely on a bank or payment company to keep the records, approve transactions, and make sure no one spends the same money twice. That arrangement works, but it means everyone must trust the middleman, pay its fees, and accept that it can delay, block, or fail. Digital information is also easy to copy, so a purely digital cash needed some way to stop the same unit from being spent twice without a central record keeper.

## The Idea: A Shared Ledger with No Central Owner

In 2008, a person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto published a paper describing Bitcoin, and the network began operating in 2009. Instead of one institution keeping the ledger, thousands of computers keep identical copies and follow shared rules, using cryptography and a consensus process to agree on which transactions are valid. Once a transaction is recorded, it is extremely difficult to change, which is what allows strangers to rely on it without trusting each other.

## Beyond Payments

The same basic idea has been extended to other uses. Blockchains such as Ethereum, launched in 2015, allow programs called smart contracts to run on the shared ledger, and many other tokens represent things like access to a service or a claim on an asset. Some of these uses are practical today and others remain experimental, so it helps to separate the technology from the price of any particular coin.

## Why People Buy Them Today

People buy cryptocurrencies for several reasons: speculation on rising prices, a belief that a limited-supply coin like Bitcoin can work as a store of value, access to applications built on a blockchain, and a wish to hold something outside the traditional banking system. Critics point to extreme price swings, the lack of cash flows behind most coins, and their use in scams and illicit finance, so the case for them remains debated.

## Example

A worker sends $500 to family abroad (illustrative costs).

**Bank wire**

- Sending fee: $25
- Receiving bank fee: $15
- Exchange-rate margin: 3% × $500 = $15
- Time: about 3 days

$$
\$25 + \$15 + \$15 = \$55 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\$55}{\$500} = 11\%
$$

**Cryptocurrency**

- Network fee: $2
- Converting in and out of local currency: 1% each way, so 2% × $500 = $10
- Time: about 1 hour

$$
\$2 + \$10 = \$12 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\$12}{\$500} = 2.4\%
$$

**The new risk: the coin's price in the hour between**

$$
3\% \text{ price drop} \times \$500 = \$15 \text{ lost}
$$

- Crypto is cheaper and faster than the bank in this case, and it saves up to $43.
- A 3% drop in the hour erases a third of that saving. A rise would add to it.

The technology removes the middlemen's fees, but the sender must convert in and out and carry the coin's price risk while the money is in transit.
