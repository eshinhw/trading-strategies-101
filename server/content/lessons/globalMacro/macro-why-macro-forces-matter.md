## The Economy Moves Everything

Interest rates, inflation, and growth affect almost every investment at the same time. A jump in interest rates can push bond prices down, change the value of a currency, and alter how investors value stocks. A shock to oil supplies can raise inflation, hurt some companies, help others, and move currencies of oil exporters and importers in opposite directions. Because these forces cut across markets, they explain a large share of what happens in prices.

## Why Start from the Top

Rather than starting with an individual security, a macro investor first forms a view on the economy and then asks which market expresses it best. The markets involved, such as government bonds, major currencies, and stock indexes, are very large and liquid, which makes it practical to trade big positions. Investors can also profit from falling prices as well as rising ones, so a view can be expressed in either direction.

## Policy Shifts and Imbalances

Governments and central banks make decisions that change the economic landscape, from raising interest rates to running large deficits to intervening in currency markets. Economies also develop imbalances, such as persistent trade deficits or rapidly rising debt, that can eventually force an adjustment. Markets don't always price these developments correctly, and the gap between what is priced in and what actually happens is where macro investors look for opportunities.

## The Risks of the Approach

Macro views can be wrong, and even correct views can be early, since markets sometimes move against a position for a long time before the economy catches up. Data can also be revised after release, and positions are often leveraged, which magnifies losses. Because a single theme can drive many positions at once, a wrong call can hurt across a whole portfolio.

## Example

An investor believes a central bank will raise rates faster than markets expect, and compares three ways to express it (illustrative, $10 million each).

**1. Sell government bonds (duration 8, yields rise 0.5%)**

$$
\$10{,}000{,}000 \times 8 \times 0.5\% = +\$400{,}000
$$

**2. Buy the country's currency (rises 2%)**

$$
\$10{,}000{,}000 \times 2\% = +\$200{,}000
$$

**3. Buy bank stocks (gain 3% as lending margins widen)**

$$
\$10{,}000{,}000 \times 3\% = +\$300{,}000
$$

**What if the market had already priced most of the move?**

- Bonds: if yields had already risen 0.4% of the 0.5%, only 0.1% is left, so the gain is $80,000.
- The currency and bank stocks may not have priced it at all.

One view moves every asset class, and the best vehicle depends on what each price already reflects and how much risk the investor wants. A later lesson covers turning a view into a trade.
