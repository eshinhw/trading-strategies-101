## The Lender's Problem

A bank that makes mortgages, car loans, or credit card loans ties up its money for years, because each loan is repaid slowly. Its capacity to lend is limited by how much capital it has, and its loans are often concentrated among borrowers in one region or industry. A lender that could only keep its loans would be able to make fewer of them and would carry all of the risk itself.

## Turning Loans into Securities

Securitization solves this by pooling many loans together and selling securities that are paid from the pool's combined cash flows. The lender receives cash from investors right away, which it can use to make new loans, and the investors receive the payments that borrowers make over time. Because the lender sells the loans rather than holding them, this approach is sometimes called originate-to-distribute.

## Spreading and Tailoring Risk

Securitization moves credit risk from the lender to investors who are willing to hold it, such as pension funds, insurers, and asset managers. The pool can also be split into slices, called tranches, with different levels of risk and return, so that a cautious investor can buy a safer slice and a risk-seeking investor a riskier one. How that splitting works is covered in the next lesson.

## More Credit, with a Catch

By connecting lenders to a much larger pool of investors, securitization can make credit cheaper and more widely available. It also has a downside: if lenders sell almost every loan they make, they may care less about the quality of each one. That weakness in incentives was one of the lessons of the 2007 to 2009 financial crisis, when poor-quality mortgages were bundled into securities and investors lost heavily.

## In Practice

A bank makes 1,000 mortgages worth 300 million dollars in total. It pools them and sells securities backed by the borrowers' monthly payments, raising roughly 300 million dollars from investors. The bank uses that cash to make new mortgages and earns fees for arranging the deal, while the investors now own claims on the mortgage payments, and the bank no longer has the loans, or most of the risk, on its books.
