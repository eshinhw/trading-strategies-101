## Raising Capital Without Borrowing

A company that wants to build a factory or develop a product needs money before it earns any. It can borrow, which means promising fixed interest payments and repaying the principal no matter how the business does, or it can sell ownership. Selling shares raises money with no repayment date and no fixed interest, and the investors who provide it accept that they will only be paid if the business does well.

## Why Investors Buy Shares

Shareholders own a claim on a company's future profits, which can reach them as dividends or as a higher share price as the business grows. Over long periods, stocks have tended to earn higher returns than lending money to the same companies, though with bigger swings along the way, and that higher potential return is the reward for taking on more risk. Shares also carry ownership rights, such as a vote on major company decisions.

## Why a Public Market Matters

When a company first sells shares to the public, in an initial public offering (IPO), it raises money in what's called the primary market. After that, investors mostly trade shares with each other in the secondary market, and the company receives nothing from those trades. The secondary market still matters to the company, because investors are far more willing to buy shares they know they can sell later, which makes it cheaper for the company to raise money in the first place.

## Dividing and Spreading Ownership

Shares let the ownership of a very large company be divided into millions of small, equal pieces, so no single investor has to fund the whole business. Shareholders also have limited liability, meaning the most they can lose is the amount they invested, even if the company fails. Spreading both the money and the risk across many people is what allows companies to grow far larger than any one investor could finance.

## Example

A company needs $200 million to build new plants and already has 40 million shares outstanding.

**Selling shares in an IPO**

- New shares: 10 million at $20 each

$$
10{,}000{,}000 \times \$20 = \boxed{\$200{,}000{,}000 \text{ raised}}
$$

**Ownership after the IPO**

$$
\frac{10}{40 + 10} = 20\% \text{ owned by the new shareholders}
$$

**Compared with borrowing the $200 million at 6%**

$$
\$200{,}000{,}000 \times 6\% = \$12{,}000{,}000 \text{ of interest every year}, \text{ plus repaying the principal}
$$

With shares, the company has no loan to repay and no fixed interest. In exchange, it shares future profits with the new owners.

**Later trading**

A buyer can sell 1,000 shares to another investor at the price the market sets, for example $24:

$$
1{,}000 \times (\$24 - \$20) = +\$4{,}000
$$

The company is not involved in that sale and receives nothing from it.
