## A Tangible Asset That Produces Income

Unlike a share or a bond, a property is a physical asset that people need to use, and tenants pay for that use through rent. That makes real estate an income-producing asset, with returns that come from rent as well as from any change in the property's value. For many investors, the appeal is having a steady flow of cash rather than depending only on a price going up.

## Some Protection Against Inflation

Over long periods, rents and property values have tended to rise along with the general price level, because the cost of buildings, land, and labor goes up with inflation and landlords can raise rents as leases renew. This is a tendency rather than a guarantee, and it varies by property type and location, but it is one reason investors use real estate to preserve purchasing power. The inflation hedging strategy in this course looks at it more closely.

## Diversification

Real estate returns don't move in lockstep with stocks and bonds, because they depend on local supply and demand, rents, and interest rates rather than on company earnings. Adding it to a portfolio of stocks and bonds can therefore reduce the portfolio's overall swings. The benefit is real but not complete, since real estate values can also fall when the economy weakens or interest rates rise sharply.

## The Trade-offs

Property is expensive to buy and sell, with transaction costs, legal fees, and taxes, and it can take months to sell, so it is much less liquid than stocks. Direct owners also take on the work of managing tenants and maintenance, or pay someone to do it. Because properties are usually bought with borrowed money, as covered in a later lesson, both gains and losses are magnified.

## Example

An investor has a $500,000 portfolio of stocks and bonds and wants steadier income and some protection against rising prices. The investor puts $100,000 into a REIT.

- REIT distribution: 5% a year, which is $5,000
- Rents rise 3% a year, so the distribution grows with them
- For comparison, $100,000 in a bond paying a fixed 4% coupon, which is $4,000 a year and does not grow

**Income after 10 years**

$$
\text{REIT: } \$5{,}000 \times 1.03^{10} = \boxed{\$6{,}720 \text{ a year}}
$$

$$
\text{Bond: } \$4{,}000 \text{ a year (unchanged)}
$$

The REIT started $1,000 ahead and the gap widens to $2,720 a year, as rents rise with prices.

**The costs of the choice**

- The value of the REIT can fall. A 20% drop would cost $20,000 on the $100,000.
- Direct property takes months to sell. Selling REIT shares takes a day, but the price can differ from the underlying property values.

Real estate adds income that grows with inflation and diversifies stocks and bonds, and the price of that is price risk and, for direct holdings, a slow sale.
