## Treasury Bills

Treasury bills (T-bills) are short-term government debt, maturing anywhere from a few days to a year, sold at a discount to face value rather than paying a stated coupon — the return is simply the gap between what an investor pays and the face value received at maturity. Backed by the government, they're treated as essentially risk-free.

## Commercial Paper

Commercial paper is short-term, unsecured debt issued directly by corporations to fund near-term needs like payroll or inventory, typically maturing in under 270 days. Because it relies on the issuing company's own creditworthiness rather than a government guarantee, it pays a higher yield than T-bills to compensate for that added risk.

## Certificates of Deposit

A certificate of deposit (CD) is a time deposit at a bank: the depositor agrees to leave funds in place for a fixed term in exchange for a fixed interest rate, typically higher than a regular savings account, with a penalty for withdrawing early. In the U.S., CDs are FDIC-insured up to statutory limits, adding a further layer of safety.

## Money Market Funds

A money market fund pools investor cash into a diversified basket of instruments like these, aiming to offer same-day liquidity and a stable share price (conventionally $1) while paying a modest yield. That stability isn't a guarantee, though — money market funds have occasionally "broken the buck," falling below their target price during periods of severe market stress, a real risk worth knowing even though it's rare.

## In Practice

A corporate treasurer managing several months of operating cash rarely leaves it all in a checking account or puts it all in a single instrument. Instead, they typically ladder it across T-bills and CDs with staggered maturities, so something is always coming due to meet upcoming obligations, while the rest keeps earning a bit more yield than sitting idle.

   > T-bills don't pay a stated coupon — the discount-to-face-value structure is itself how the return is earned.

   - Commercial paper is risk-free, just like a T-bill
   - Yield has nothing to do with credit risk for short-term instruments
   > Since commercial paper is unsecured corporate debt rather than a government obligation, investors demand extra yield to compensate for that added credit risk.

   - There is never any penalty for early withdrawal

   > A CD's higher rate is offered in exchange for committing funds for the stated term — withdrawing early typically triggers a penalty that claws back some of that benefit.

4. What does it mean for a money market fund to "break the buck"?
   - [x] Its share price falls below its conventional $1 target during a period of severe market stress
   - It pays out more than $1 per share to every investor

   - It is a routine, daily event for every money market fund
   > Money market funds aim for share-price stability, but that stability isn't guaranteed — a fund's value can occasionally slip below its target during severe stress.

5. Why might a corporate treasurer ladder T-bills and CDs across staggered maturities rather than pick one instrument?
   - [x] So something is always maturing to meet upcoming obligations while the rest keeps earning yield
   - Laddering is required by law for all corporate cash
   - It guarantees a higher return than any single instrument
   - There is no benefit to staggering maturities
   > Laddering balances liquidity and yield: near-term needs are covered as instruments mature on schedule, while the rest of the cash isn't sitting completely idle.
