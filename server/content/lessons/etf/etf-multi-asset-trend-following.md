## One Rule Across Many Asset Classes

Multi-asset trend following takes a simple trend-following rule, like a moving-average crossover, and applies it identically across a broad basket of ETFs spanning many different, largely uncorrelated asset classes: equities, bonds, commodities, real estate, and currencies, for example, each represented by a liquid ETF proxy rather than requiring direct access to futures or other specialized markets in each asset class.

## Diversifying Trend Opportunity

The logic for spreading a single trading rule across so many different asset classes is diversification of trend opportunity: any one asset class might be trendless or choppy at a given time, but with a broad enough basket, there's a good chance that at least some of the ETFs in the basket are in a genuine, tradeable trend at any given moment, and the strategy's overall return depends on capturing those trends wherever they happen to show up, rather than betting on trends appearing in any one specific market.

## A Smoother Return Profile

Because the underlying asset classes are relatively uncorrelated with each other, day-to-day and month-to-month, a diversified multi-asset trend portfolio also tends to have a smoother overall return profile than applying the same trend rule to just one or two closely related markets — a trend-following equity ETF position losing money doesn't necessarily mean a trend-following bond or commodity ETF position is losing money at the same time, since they're often responding to different underlying drivers.

## Performing Well in Equity Crises

Multi-asset trend following has historically shown a useful property during equity market crises: because trends can also form during equity selloffs, a sustained downtrend is still a trend, and the strategy can go short or move to cash on the equity leg while simultaneously catching a rising trend in, say, government bonds or gold, the strategy has sometimes performed well precisely during periods when a simple buy-and-hold equity portfolio performs poorly, which is part of why it is often framed as a portfolio diversifier rather than purely a source of standalone returns.
