## The Expense Ratio

The expense ratio is the annual fee an ETF charges, expressed as a percentage of the assets invested. It isn't billed to the investor, it's deducted from the fund's assets gradually through the year, so it shows up as slightly lower returns. An expense ratio of 0.10% costs about 10 dollars a year on a 10,000 dollar investment, and because it's charged every year, it matters more the longer an investor holds.

## Bid-Ask Spread and Trading Costs

Every time an investor buys or sells an ETF, they trade at the bid or the ask, not at the midpoint, so each round trip costs the spread. For a large, heavily traded ETF the spread can be a fraction of a cent, while for a thinly traded ETF it can be much wider. Unlike the expense ratio, this cost is paid at the time of each trade, which makes it more important for investors who trade often.

## Tracking Difference and Tracking Error

An index fund aims to match its index, but it never does so exactly. Tracking difference is the gap between the ETF's return and the index's return over a period, and it is usually close to the expense ratio, though things like holding a representative sample of securities, small cash balances, and income from lending securities can push it either way. Tracking error measures how consistent that gap is: it is commonly calculated as the standard deviation of the return differences, so a low tracking error means the ETF follows its index closely and predictably.

## Putting the Costs Together

The real cost of owning an ETF is the expense ratio paid each year plus the spread paid when buying and selling, plus any tracking difference beyond the fee. Over a short holding period, the spread can dominate, while over many years the expense ratio matters more. Comparing two ETFs on the same index therefore means looking at all of these together, not just the quoted fee.

## Example

Two ETFs track the same index, and an investor puts in $10,000.

- Fund A: expense ratio 0.03%, spread cost 0.01% per trade
- Fund B: expense ratio 0.20%, spread cost 0.10% per trade (thinly traded)

**Annual fees**

$$
\text{Fund A: } \$10{,}000 \times 0.03\% = \$3 \qquad \text{Fund B: } \$10{,}000 \times 0.20\% = \$20
$$

$$
\$20 - \$3 = \boxed{\$17 \text{ more a year}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 5 \times \$17 = \$85 \text{ over five years (ignoring compounding)}
$$

**Spread cost for an investor who trades often (4 round trips a year, which is 8 trades)**

$$
\text{Fund A: } 8 \times \$10{,}000 \times 0.01\% = \$8 \qquad \text{Fund B: } 8 \times \$10{,}000 \times 0.10\% = \$80
$$

**Total first-year cost for that investor**

$$
\text{Fund A: } \$3 + \$8 = \$11 \qquad \text{Fund B: } \$20 + \$80 = \$100
$$

Fund B costs about nine times as much, so Fund A is cheaper on both counts unless Fund B offers something different, such as exposure to another index.
