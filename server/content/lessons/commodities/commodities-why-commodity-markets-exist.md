## Prices That Swing

The price of a raw material can change sharply because of weather, wars, shifts in demand, or a single disruption to supply. A farmer planting wheat in the spring doesn't know what it will sell for at harvest, and an airline planning its schedule doesn't know what jet fuel will cost in six months. Both are running businesses whose profit can depend as much on a commodity's price as on how well they operate.

## Price Discovery

A commodity market brings many buyers and sellers together in one place, so what they collectively know and expect gets reflected in a single, visible price. That price then becomes a reference for the whole world: benchmark crude oil prices such as WTI and Brent are used in contracts everywhere. Without a central market, each deal would be negotiated privately with little information about what a fair price is.

## Hedging Price Risk

Commodity markets let producers and consumers reduce their price risk. A producer can sell futures to lock in the price of what it will produce, and a consumer can buy futures to lock in the cost of what it will need. This is the same idea as the forward contract covered in the Forwards course, carried out on an exchange with standardized contracts that anyone can trade.

## Speculators and Investors Provide Liquidity

Hedgers alone don't always match up, since a farmer who wants to sell wheat forward may not find an airline that wants to buy it. Speculators step in and take the other side of the trade in the hope of profit, and investors hold commodities for diversification or as protection against inflation. Their trading makes the market more liquid, which lowers the cost of hedging for everyone else.

## Example

**An airline hedges fuel**

- Annual fuel use: 120 million gallons
- Hedged: 75%, which is 90 million gallons, at $2.50 a gallon with oil-related futures
- Locked-in cost on the hedged part: 90 million × $2.50 = $225 million

If fuel rises to $3.00:

$$
\text{Extra cost on all fuel} = 120 \times (\$3.00 - \$2.50) = \$60 \text{ million}
$$

$$
\text{Futures gain} = 90 \times (\$3.00 - \$2.50) = +\$45 \text{ million}
$$

$$
\$60 \text{ million} - \$45 \text{ million} = \boxed{\$15 \text{ million net cost}}
$$

**A farmer hedges the harvest**

- Sells 100,000 bushels of wheat futures at $6.00, locking in $600,000

**The speculator on the other side**

A speculator buys those futures. If wheat rises to $7.00, the speculator gains (7.00 − 6.00) × 100,000 = $100,000, and if it falls to $5.00 the speculator loses $100,000.

The airline and farmer remove price from their budgets. The speculator is paid, with the chance of profit, for carrying the risk they wanted to avoid.
