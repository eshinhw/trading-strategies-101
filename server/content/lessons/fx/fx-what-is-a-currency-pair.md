## Trading Two Currencies at Once

Unlike a stock, which has a single price, a currency only has a value relative to another currency — you can't simply buy "a dollar," only a dollar in exchange for some other currency. A currency pair, like EUR/USD, expresses exactly that relationship: how much of the second currency it takes to buy one unit of the first.

## Base and Quote Currency

In a pair like EUR/USD, the first currency listed, EUR, is the base currency, and the second, USD, is the quote currency. The quoted price tells you how many units of the quote currency one unit of the base currency is worth — a EUR/USD price of 1.10 means one euro buys $1.10.

## Going Long or Short a Pair

Going long a currency pair means buying the base currency and simultaneously selling the quote currency, profiting if the base strengthens relative to the quote. Going short does the reverse — selling the base and buying the quote, profiting if the base weakens instead.

## Major, Minor, and Exotic Pairs

Pairs are commonly grouped by how heavily they trade: major pairs involve the U.S. dollar and another large, freely-traded currency (like EUR/USD or USD/JPY) and are the most liquid; minor pairs pair two major currencies without the dollar (like EUR/GBP); and exotic pairs involve a major currency against a smaller or less liquid one (like USD/TRY), typically trading with wider spreads and less liquidity.

## In Practice

A U.S. tourist arriving in Japan and exchanging dollars for yen at the airport is executing, in miniature, exactly what a currency pair describes: they're simultaneously selling their base currency (USD) and buying the quote currency (JPY) at whatever the USD/JPY rate happens to be that day, and the number of yen they walk away with depends entirely on where that pair is trading at the moment of the exchange.
