## Three Basic Uses

Everything done in the FX market falls into one of three broad purposes: transacting (converting money because you need another currency), hedging (reducing the risk that a currency move will hurt you), and speculating or investing (taking on currency risk deliberately in the hope of earning a return). Knowing which purpose is behind a trade explains why someone would take a position that, viewed on its own, can look strange.

## Transacting: Paying and Getting Paid

The simplest use of a currency is to convert it so you can pay for something or because you've just been paid in it. An importer buys the supplier's currency to settle an invoice, an exporter sells the foreign currency it received, and a traveler exchanges money before a trip. These trades aren't bets on where the exchange rate is heading, they're just the mechanics of moving money across borders.

## Hedging: Taking Risk Off

A business or investor that already has exposure to a foreign currency, because it will receive or owe that currency later or owns assets denominated in it, can use FX trades to offset the risk. The goal is to make a future amount certain rather than to make a profit, and the price of that certainty is giving up the benefit if the exchange rate moves in your favor. Hedging is covered in more detail later in this course.

## Speculating and Investing: Taking Risk On

Others trade currencies because they hold a view and want exposure to it. A trader who expects the euro to strengthen can buy EUR/USD, and an investor can hold a higher-yielding currency to collect the interest rate difference, which is the idea behind the carry trade covered in the strategies section of this course. Here the currency exposure is the point, not a side effect.

## One Trade, Different Purposes

Nothing about a trade itself reveals its purpose: buying euros with dollars could be an importer paying a bill, a fund manager hedging, or a trader betting on the euro. What differs is the surrounding position. For a hedger the trade offsets an exposure they already have, while for a speculator it creates a new one, and that distinction shapes how much risk each participant is actually running.

## Example

A U.S. company expects to be paid €2,000,000 in three months. EUR/USD spot is 1.1000, and the three-month forward rate is 1.1055.

**Doing nothing (unhedged)**

$$
\text{EUR/USD at 1.05: } 2{,}000{,}000 \times 1.05 = \$2{,}100{,}000
$$

$$
\text{EUR/USD at 1.15: } 2{,}000{,}000 \times 1.15 = \$2{,}300{,}000
$$

The company receives $200,000 more or less depending on where the rate lands.

**Hedging: sell the euros forward today**

$$
2{,}000{,}000 \times 1.1055 = \boxed{\$2{,}211{,}000 \text{ in every case}}
$$

**A trader sells €2,000,000 forward at 1.1055 with no euro receivable**

The trade is identical, but there is nothing to offset it.

$$
\text{EUR/USD at 1.05: } (1.1055 - 1.05) \times 2{,}000{,}000 = +\$111{,}000
$$

$$
\text{EUR/USD at 1.15: } (1.1055 - 1.15) \times 2{,}000{,}000 = -\$89{,}000
$$

The same trade removes $200,000 of risk for the company and creates $200,000 of risk for the trader. The purpose is what separates a hedge from a bet.

# Quiz

1. Which of these is NOT one of the three basic reasons people trade currencies?
   - Transacting, to pay for or receive something in another currency
   - Hedging, to reduce currency risk
   - Speculating or investing, to profit from currency moves
   - [x] Printing new currency to fund government spending
   > Currency trading is used to transact, hedge, or speculate and invest, while printing money is a government function, not a market activity.

2. Why would an importer buy a foreign currency?
   - [x] To settle an invoice owed in that currency, which is a transaction need rather than a bet on the exchange rate
   - To speculate that the exchange rate will fall
   - Because buying currency is required by law every month
   - To avoid ever paying the supplier
   > Paying a foreign supplier requires the supplier's currency, which is simply the mechanics of cross-border payment.

3. What is the main trade-off of hedging a currency exposure?
   - Hedging always increases profit
   - Hedging has no cost and no downside
   - [x] It makes a future amount more certain, but gives up the benefit if the exchange rate moves in your favor
   - Hedging guarantees a profit on every trade
   > A hedge removes uncertainty in both directions, which protects against losses but also forgoes gains.

4. In a speculative or investing use of currencies, how does the currency exposure differ from a hedge?
   - It is an accidental side effect
   - It is always zero
   - It is illegal
   - [x] It is the point of the trade, taken on deliberately in hope of a return
   > A speculator or investor wants currency exposure and takes it on purpose, while a hedger is trying to remove an exposure they already have.

5. A trader sells euros forward but has no euros to receive. Compared with a company hedging a euro receivable, what is the trade?
   - A different trade with a different price
   - Impossible to place
   - [x] Economically the same trade, but it creates new currency risk rather than offsetting existing risk
   - A trade with no risk at all
   > The trade can be identical, and what changes is the surrounding position: a hedger offsets an existing exposure while a speculator creates a new one.

6. {#calc1} [calc] A company expects €1,500,000 and sells it forward at 1.1055. At maturity the spot rate is 1.1500. What is the company's cost or benefit compared with not hedging?
   - It gains $66,750
   - It gives up $44,500
   - [x] It gives up $66,750
   - It gains $44,500
   > Hedged: 1,500,000 × 1.1055 = $1,658,250. Unhedged: 1,500,000 × 1.15 = $1,725,000. The hedge gives up $66,750 of upside in exchange for certainty.
