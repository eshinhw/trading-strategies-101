## What Theta Measures

Theta is the change in an option's price as one day passes, with everything else held constant. Because time value shrinks as expiration approaches, a long option has a negative theta: a theta of −0.05 means the option loses about 0.05 per share each day, or 5 dollars per contract, even if the stock doesn't move. The seller of the same option has a positive theta, because the decay is the seller's gain.

## The Shape of Time Decay

Time decay isn't a straight line. An option with a long time to expiration loses value slowly at first, and the decay speeds up as expiration nears, especially for at-the-money options. In the last few weeks the daily loss is much larger than it was months earlier, which is why short-dated options are cheap to hold for a day only if the stock moves quickly.

## Which Options Decay Fastest

At-the-money options have the highest theta, because they carry the most time value to lose. Deep in-the-money and far out-of-the-money options have less time value, so they decay more slowly in dollar terms. An option that finishes out of the money loses all of its remaining time value by expiration.

## Theta Works Against Buyers

Option buyers need the stock to move enough, and soon enough, to outrun time decay. A call that gains from a rise in the stock can still lose value overall if the rise is too slow, because theta takes away a little every day. This is the main cost of the limited-risk, high-leverage nature of buying options.

## Theta Works for Sellers

Sellers of options, such as covered call writers or iron condor sellers, collect the decay as income if the stock stays calm. The risk is on the other side: in exchange for positive theta they accept negative gamma, so a large move against the position can cost far more than the decay earned. Selling options is essentially being paid to take on that risk.

## Example

A trader buys a call for 4.00 with a theta of −0.06. If the stock stays exactly where it is, the call loses about 0.06 a day, so after a week it is worth about 3.58, a loss of 42 dollars per contract with no change in the stock price. A seller of the same call would have gained that 42 dollars, which shows why timing matters as much as direction for an option buyer.
