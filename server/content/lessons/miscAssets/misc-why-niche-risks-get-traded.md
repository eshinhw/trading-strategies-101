## Risks That Don't Fit Standard Markets

Many businesses face risks that no ordinary security covers. A ski resort loses revenue in a warm winter, a power plant is hurt when gas prices rise faster than electricity prices, and a pension fund promising inflation-adjusted benefits loses if inflation surprises on the upside. These are real financial exposures, but there is no stock or bond whose price tracks them directly.

## Risk Transfer

Instruments built for these exposures let the business transfer part of the risk to someone else. The hedger pays, usually through a premium or a fixed leg of a swap, to pass the risk on, and the other side accepts it in return for expected compensation. For the counterparty, such risks can also be attractive because they often move independently of stocks and bonds, which can diversify a portfolio.

## Why These Markets Are Small

Because each exposure is specific, the contracts are often tailored, traded by a small set of participants, and not very liquid. Few investors know how to price a temperature index or a spark spread, and trading volumes are modest compared with stocks or bonds. This is why such instruments are grouped as miscellaneous: they don't belong to a standard asset class and exist mainly to solve a particular problem.

## Hedging Is Rarely Perfect

A hedge only works to the extent that the instrument's payout matches the actual loss. A temperature index measured at an airport might not match the weather at a particular resort, and an inflation index may not match a firm's own costs. This mismatch is called basis risk, and it is one reason spread and basis trading, covered later in this course, matters for these markets.

## Example

A ski resort earns less when winters are mild. It buys a weather derivative that pays out if a temperature index shows a warmer winter than a set level, so a bad season is partly offset by the payout. The counterparty might be an investor or insurer who accepts the risk in return for a premium, and who values it because weather is largely unrelated to what happens in the stock market.
