## Hedging: Protecting What You Own

An investor who owns a stock can buy a put option to set a floor under it. If the stock falls, the put gains value and offsets the loss, and if the stock rises, the investor keeps the gain minus the cost of the put. A collar goes one step further by selling a call to help pay for the put, trading some upside for cheaper protection. These are covered in the stock and option strategies later in this course.

## Generating Income: Selling Premium

An investor who owns a stock can sell a call against it, collecting a premium in exchange for agreeing to sell the shares at a higher price. Selling a put is similar, collecting a premium while agreeing to buy the stock if it falls to the strike. These approaches earn money when the stock doesn't move much, and the cost is that the potential gain is capped or a loss is possible if the stock moves the wrong way.

## Speculating with Limited Risk

A trader who expects a stock to rise can buy a call instead of the stock, and one who expects it to fall can buy a put. The advantage is that the most that can be lost is the premium, while a stock position can lose far more. The cost is that the option has an expiration date, so the view has to prove right within a limited time.

## Trading Volatility and Ranges

More advanced strategies don't depend on direction at all. A long straddle makes money if the stock moves a lot in either direction, while an iron condor makes money if it stays in a range. These express views about how much a stock will move rather than which way, which is something only options can do directly.

## Matching the Tool to the View

Choosing a strategy comes down to a few questions: what is the view on direction, how large and how fast is the expected move, and how much risk is acceptable. A mild bullish view may suit a bull call spread, while a strong one may suit a long call. The strategies later in this course are organized from the simplest forms to the most complex, so each one can be understood as a combination of simpler pieces.

## In Practice

Three investors hold the same view of a stock trading at 100 but have different goals. One already owns it and buys a put to protect against a drop, one owns it and sells a call to earn income while expecting it to stay flat, and one doesn't own it but expects a rise and buys a call to profit with limited risk. They use the same type of instrument for three different purposes.

   > A straddle profits from the size of the move, not its direction.
