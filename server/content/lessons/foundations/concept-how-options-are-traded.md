## Listed, Standardized Contracts

Most options are listed on exchanges, which means the terms are standardized: a fixed set of strike prices and expiration dates for each stock. A clearinghouse, the Options Clearing Corporation in the United States, stands behind every contract and guarantees it, so a buyer doesn't need to worry about the seller's ability to pay. This standardization is what lets contracts be bought and sold freely before expiration.

## Contract Size and Quoting

One option contract normally covers 100 shares of the underlying stock. Prices are quoted per share, so a quote of 3.00 means one contract costs 3.00 × 100 = 300 dollars, plus any commission. This is why a small quoted number can represent a meaningful amount of money, and why it is important to multiply by 100 when working out a position.

## The Option Chain

An option chain is a table that lists the available options on a stock. For each expiration date it shows the strikes, with calls on one side and puts on the other, and for each option it gives the bid, the ask, the volume traded, and the open interest, which is the number of contracts currently outstanding. A buyer pays the ask, a seller receives the bid, and the gap between them is a cost of trading, so options with high volume and open interest are usually easier to trade.

## Expiration and Exercise

Options on individual U.S. stocks are generally American style, meaning the holder can exercise at any time up to expiration, while many index options are European style and can be exercised only at expiration. In practice most traders close their positions by selling or buying back the option before expiration rather than exercising. A seller can be assigned, which means the buyer exercised and the seller must deliver the shares or the cash, so sellers need to be prepared for that.

## Margin and Account Approval

Brokers approve accounts for different levels of options trading depending on experience and risk. Buying an option requires paying the full premium, while selling an option exposes the seller to larger losses, so the broker requires collateral called margin. Strategies with defined risk, like spreads, usually need less margin than selling options with no protection.

## In Practice

An investor looks at the option chain for a stock trading at 100 and sees the 100-strike call quoted at a bid of 3.90 and an ask of 4.10. Buying one contract costs the ask, 4.10 × 100 = 410 dollars. If the investor wanted to sell it right away, only the bid of 3.90 would be received, which is 390 dollars, so crossing the spread costs about 20 dollars. That small gap is the price of trading immediately.
