## Protection Without Giving Up the Upside

An investor who owns a stock faces a dilemma when worried about a drop. Selling removes the risk but also gives up any further gains, and may trigger taxes, while holding keeps both the upside and the downside. An option offers a third choice: pay a small amount for protection that only matters if the stock falls, and keep the shares for the case where it rises. It works much like insurance on a house, where the premium is the cost of being covered.

## A Right Without an Obligation

The buyer of an option pays a premium for a right but not an obligation, so the most the buyer can lose is the premium paid. The seller takes on the obligation in exchange for receiving the premium. That asymmetry creates payoffs that can't be copied by owning or shorting the stock, such as a position with limited downside and unlimited upside.

## Who Uses Options and Why

Hedgers use options to protect an existing position from losses. Income seekers sell options to collect premium, accepting some risk in return for regular cash. Speculators use options to take a view on direction or volatility with a known maximum loss, and market makers quote prices on both sides to keep trading smooth. The same contract can serve any of these purposes depending on who holds it.

## Leverage and Flexibility

One option contract controls 100 shares for a fraction of what the shares would cost, so a small premium can produce a large percentage gain or loss. Options can also express views that stocks can't: a bet that a stock will move a lot without saying which way, that it will stay in a range, or that it will rise but not above a certain level. This flexibility is why the course covers so many different strategies.

## How Options Became Standardized

Agreements giving someone the right to buy or sell at a set price have existed for a very long time, but they were private deals that were hard to price or exit. In 1973 the Chicago Board Options Exchange opened and began listing standardized contracts, backed by a clearinghouse that guarantees each trade. Standard terms and a central guarantee made options easy to trade and liquid, and that is the market investors use today.

## Example

A fund manager holds 100,000 shares at $50, which is $5,000,000, and worries about an earnings report next week.

- Protection: buy 1,000 put contracts (100,000 shares) with a strike of 48 at 1.50
- Cost: 100,000 × $1.50 = $150,000, which is 3% of the position

**If the report disappoints and the stock falls 20% to $40**

$$
\text{Stock: } -\$10 \times 100{,}000 = -\$1{,}000{,}000
$$

$$
\text{Puts: } (\$48 - \$40) \times 100{,}000 = +\$800{,}000
$$

$$
-\$1{,}000{,}000 + \$800{,}000 - \$150{,}000 = \boxed{-\$350{,}000 \text{ instead of } -\$1{,}000{,}000}
$$

**If the stock rises 20% to $60**

$$
+\$1{,}000{,}000 - \$150{,}000 = +\$850{,}000
$$

**Compared with selling the stock**

Selling would lock in the result and could trigger a tax bill, and doing nothing would leave the full $1,000,000 loss exposed. The puts limit the loss to $350,000 while keeping the upside, for a known cost of $150,000.
